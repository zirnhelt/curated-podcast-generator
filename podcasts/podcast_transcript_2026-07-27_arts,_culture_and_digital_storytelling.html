Casey: Netflix chasing TikTok's format tells you something important. It's not that audiences suddenly decided they like worse video. It's that engagement metrics reward a specific shape, and now even the biggest studio on the planet is bending its own product to fit it.

Riley: Or it tells you the audience already moved and Netflix is just catching up to where people actually are. A HubSpot report from last year found vertical short-form was the single most-used and most successful content format in all of marketing, full stop. That's not a taste judgment — it's a distribution fact. And for a musician or a small theatre company way out here, that fact matters more than any critic's opinion about format snobbery.

Casey: Distribution fact for whom, though. A fifteen-second vertical clip of a three-chord hook works because it's engineered to be consumed in fifteen seconds. Live theatre isn't built that way. A folk trio playing a forty-minute set at Boitano Park doesn't compress into a hook and a caption without losing what actually makes it good.

Riley: Nobody's proposing you replace the performance with the clip. The clip is the door, not the room. Casey: Some fraction. What's the actual conversion rate on that fraction? Because the honest answer, in every serious study of viral reach, is that most of those impressions evaporate without ever turning into a ticket sale or an album purchase. Vertical video builds impressions. It doesn't reliably build audiences.

Riley: That's true of any single clip in isolation. It's much less true of a body of work posted consistently over an entire season. He built weekly output on a platform he controlled, over years, and it became a genuinely large, durable reach.

Casey: Refusing outright isn't the only alternative, though. There's a third option nobody in the Verge piece even considers: build the archive and the audience on infrastructure the community actually owns, and let the platform clip be a byproduct of that, rather than the goal itself.
