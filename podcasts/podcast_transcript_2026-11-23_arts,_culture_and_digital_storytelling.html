Riley: When a valuable painting changes hands under pressure, and the sale is later disputed, does a settlement close the question? Or should the record of how it left its owner stay open, and who should keep it? Put another way: does the work of holding a record belong to whoever is still standing in the room, or does it need someone from outside?

Casey: Stay open. The price got settled. The history didn't.

Riley: Right. Who owns the painting now is one question. How it left the family is another. The sale answered the first one.

Casey: The painting is Van Gogh's Châtaigniers en fleurs, from 1890. The heirs of Samuel Fischer, a German-Jewish publisher, say the family sold it in Amsterdam in 1936 under Nazi persecution. They asked Sotheby's to pull it from the November 18th sale in New York. The house had valued it at $180 million, the highest estimate for any lot that season.

Riley: On November 17th, Sotheby's announced a "mutually satisfactory resolution" between the current consignors, the Blaquier-Arrieta family, and the legal descendants of Hedwig and Samuel Fischer. The work sold under that agreement. The claim went public days before the sale and was settled before the hammer fell. That's fast.

Casey: Fast, and short. The terms weren't disclosed. The public got a paragraph and no document.

Riley: A paragraph is what a settlement usually looks like. The speed is the point. Pressure on a market works when the claim is visible, and a claim made through press and online campaigns moved a $180 million lot.

Casey: The Guardian put the sale at $190.4 million, a record for a Van Gogh. The Telegraph reported about £145 million. The family's lawyer, Schnabel, said the family hoped the attention would lead to a "fast and fair and just resolution." Two outlets, two currencies, one sale. Whether the resolution was fair, nobody outside the agreement can check.

Riley: Then the buyer paid a record in public, with the claim already on the table. Anyone bidding on provenance-heavy work now knows that question gets asked in public, and at the top of the market.

Casey: Or the seller and the buyer both got what they wanted, and the heirs got a private payment nobody can see. From outside, those two look identical. It's not even clear the heirs were paid at all.

Riley: They differ in one respect. Before this, there was one account of how the work left the family. Now there are two, the heirs' and the auction house's. Visibility is what lets anyone check either one.

Casey: Two accounts is not a record. The heirs' account comes from the family making the claim. The auction house's comes from the party selling the work. Neither one comes from someone who gains nothing from the sale.

Riley: Two interested parties can still check each other. The claim and the statement either match or they don't, and a reader can see the gap.

Casey: Only if the statement says something checkable. "Mutually satisfactory" names no price, no date and no payment. It names two parties and a verdict. That closes the dispute and leaves the route unstated.

Riley: Then the route has to be written somewhere else. The question is who does that, and whether they can afford to do it when nobody is watching.

Riley: The Internet Archive is a useful case, because keeping the record is its whole job. It runs the Wayback Machine, and its mission is what it calls "Universal Access to All Knowledge." It was founded in 1996. More than 80 of its 124 eligible U.S. staffers voted to join the Communications Workers of America, and they are waiting on founder Brewster Kahle to recognize the union voluntarily. The backup is a vote run by the U.S. National Labor Relations Board. Even a mission-driven nonprofit is now a workplace with collective bargaining, and its staff are part of the cultural infrastructure. A nonprofit keeper has no consignor to satisfy.

Casey: No consignor, but a founder. Kahle hadn't responded to WIRED's request for comment when that story ran. The people keeping the record don't have settled terms at their own institution yet, and a mission can make it harder to ask for basic workplace terms.

Riley: Staff who share the mission and can organize make the keeper stronger. They are part of the record.

Casey: Alignment with a mission doesn't tell you the record survives the next budget. The Internet Archive has 124 eligible U.S. staffers. A small collection might have one person who knows where everything is.

Riley: Which is the case for writing it down. The Eleanor Antin dispute from the roundup shows the problem. The party holding the works made the list. Somebody outside the dealer's office had to check it, and the artist's side read it closely enough to find the gap.

Casey: The check caught the gap, but the people checking still work for the artist. Nobody in that chain is neutral. The check worked because someone with a reason to look was reading.

Riley: A reason to look counts for something. Records get tested that way all the time.

Casey: Only while the reader has a job. Take the Mada Masr case from the roundup. When a licence rule decides who may publish, it also decides who keeps the archive, and a confiscation order takes the equipment with it.

Riley: Which is why copies sit outside the building. The archive that matters is the one that survives a raid, a confiscation or a closed budget line. Off-site copies are the cheap answer, and they work for a newsroom in Cairo the same way they work for a museum in Williams Lake.

Casey: The Egyptian court had a legal tool. The Cariboo has a board and a budget. Those are different levers, and the second one is easier to pull quietly.

Riley: Then name the lever. Who can see the file, who can change it, and what happens when the person who holds the key leaves. Write those three answers into the record itself.

Casey: Nieman Lab has the same problem with public media. The Corporation for Public Broadcasting dissolved, and whoever rebuilds it inherits the files and not the staff who knew them.

Riley: That's a reason to keep the staff in the record. A recovered system still needs people who remember how the files were arranged.

Casey: Agreed on that. I don't accept that a rebuilt system fixes itself. It needs a line item, the same as the one it lost.

Riley: So build the line item in. Now bring it home. The Museum of the Cariboo Chilcotin in Williams Lake is a regional history museum, and the BC Cowboy Hall of Fame sits inside it. A museum like that holds objects that came from many donors over many years. The entry for each one can be short: who gave it, when, and under what conditions. Dated, signed and reviewed. The museum already asks donors to fill out a donation form and give provenance details, so the habit exists. Any collection can adopt it and show it.

Casey: It can. But in a regional collection, some of those stories are already gone. The donor may be long gone, the paperwork may sit in a drawer, and the one person who remembered the handover may have retired. A form won't recover a story nobody wrote down. The entry should say plainly what is unknown.

Riley: An entry that says "unknown" is still a record. A blank filled with a guess is the bigger risk.

Casey: Who checks the "unknown"? If the museum writes its own entries and also holds the objects, it's the seller and the keeper in one room. Same conflict as the auction house, in a smaller room.

Riley: Then the list needs an outside reader. Public, dated, and with the gaps stated. Put it under a named keeper with a review date, and it survives the volunteer who wrote it.

Casey: Named keeper, review date. Fine. Who keeps the keeper's job when the budget line goes? That's the part I'd want written down.

Riley: The same logic reaches the shop shelf. Mama C's Gifts & Giggles and The Crafty Nook carry local makers' jewellery and honey, and each piece has a maker whose name should travel with it.

Casey: Only if somebody writes the name on the tag. Whatever a shop's first owner knew about each maker leaves with the sale, unless someone wrote it down.

Riley: Then the write-down is cheap. A name, a date and a sentence on the tag. The shelf can keep the record the way a museum can.

Casey: Cheap for the shelf. For a museum with a hundred years of donors, the sentence on the tag is the expensive part, because someone has to go find the sentence first.

Riley: My pick is the collection that holds the object, with an outside reader who can check it.

Casey: I'd put it with a neutral archive. I don't have one picked out for the Cariboo, and the seller already has a record. It's the one that produced the two-sentence announcement.
