The plan

Roundup frame: A day where money, mandates and who gets to decide run through nearly every story, from Cariboo mine financing to First Nations chiefs doubting Ottawa's motives.

Named bridges:

  • Federal money and Indigenous priorities: the chiefs' complaints about the meeting lead into what First Nations are asking of the federal budget.
  • Federal funding announced for Indigenous-linked needs: from the national budget to a specific $1 billion Winnipeg commitment that includes First Nations partners.
  • Public services for Indigenous people: from housing funding to the child protection system that Nunavut's auditor general has criticised again.
  • Trees and Indigenous land: from a plantation near a community to cottonwood restoration along BC's Nechako River.
  • Large projects on public or contested land: from LNG to data centres being pushed onto federal land.
  • Data centres: from the proposed US sites to the workers who build them in Europe.
  • Cranbrook city hall: from the mayor's view of the province to who sits for the city on regional bodies.

Handoff to the deep dive: Across these stories, decisions about land, services and money keep being made by someone other than the people who live with them, which leads into who holds that power in a Cariboo First Nation this weekend.

Deep-dive question: When a Nation in the Cariboo votes for its chief and council, and that leadership is the body that decides on community services and lands, does a member living away from the community have an equal say, and should those who have stayed and carried the work count for more than those who cannot be there?

Riley starts: Self-government works best when a Nation's own electoral process is open to every member, including those living off reserve, and that inclusion strengthens the leadership's authority to decide on lands and services.
Casey starts: A vote only matters if the leadership it selects actually holds the decision-making power over lands and services, and a ballot count says little about whether those who gave the most to the community, or those who have been shut out, are served.

  1. Esk'etemc's election is an exercise of self-government: members choose the chief and councillors who make decisions on community services and lands.
    then, because: If office-holders' power is the real question, then who gets to choose them, including members living away, bears on that power.
  2. Mail-in ballots for members living off the reservation mean the Nation counts its dispersed members as full participants in who decides.
    then, because: If the vote includes people who live elsewhere, the question becomes whether the decisions that follow should answer to residents, to all members, or to those who carried the work.
  3. Federal processes show why a Nation's own leadership matters: chiefs say Ottawa's meeting sidelined their agenda, so the authority that counts is the one the Nation itself selects.
    then, because: With external actors setting agendas, what a council can actually decide, such as language programs or harvesting access, shows what the vote is for.
  4. What a council can allocate, such as language programs or access to resources, is where reciprocity to elders and knowledge-holders competes with the needs of members at large.
    then, because: If the rule for dividing is the Nation's own to write, the Cariboo case is who in Esk'etemc's electorate writes it.

Landing: Held open: when a Nation's electorate spans residents and members living away, and the council divides things like language programs and harvesting access, who in the Nation should set the rule for how the contribution of elders and knowledge-holders weighs against wider need, and who should be able to see how it is applied.
