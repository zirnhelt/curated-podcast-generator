The plan

Roundup frame: A day of Indigenous law, land and fire stewardship, with a few hard rural-resource questions underneath.

Named bridges:

  • Federal law and who decides: from a possible rewrite of the Indian Act to three federal bills that First Nations youth and an MP say cut against consent.
  • Indigenous authority over land: from fights over federal law to Indigenous-led fire stewardship gaining public support.
  • Indigenous-led stewardship being paid for: from cultural burning to the Northwest Territories conservation trust that funds Guardians.
  • Indigenous communities and big infrastructure: from a northern conservation model to an Indigenous revolt against a dam in India.
  • AI and the people who do the work: from AI in the classroom to AI reshaping software jobs.
  • Energy infrastructure and where it gets built: from Alberta's rules on wind and solar to Germany's hydrogen pipeline.

Handoff to the deep dive: Who gets a real say before Ottawa declares a project to be in the national interest, and what a nation does when the process offered to it is not a real one.

Deep-dive question: When Ottawa has designated a coastal oil pipeline a national-interest project without consulting the nations it crosses, is a negotiator better to keep a seat in a flawed process to protect what is left of the relationship, or to go to court, and what does each choice do to the nation's standing as the one who decides?

Riley starts: Riley starts from keeping a seat at the table if the process can still be repaired, but accepts that a court challenge is a legitimate lever for a nation whose Crown partner skipped consultation entirely.
Casey starts: Casey starts from the view that taking part in a flawed process lets it be filed as consent, so a nation should refuse to lend it legitimacy and test it in court, even at the cost of goodwill.

Landing: Held open: when a Crown process is flawed from the start, who gets to say it has become one that counts as consent (the Nation, the court, or the Crown), and does going to court keep the relationship alive by forcing a real process to exist, or end it?
