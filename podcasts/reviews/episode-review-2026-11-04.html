The plan

Roundup frame: A day of local swearing-in and long-running community vigils, followed by a run of gadgets, fixes and tinkering stories about keeping devices working and in your own hands.

Handoff to the deep dive: The listener is left holding the iPhone Duo's cracked screens: the maker says repair or replace, but never says why it broke, and owners wait on someone else's parts. That sets up who controls the parts, and whether rules about where gear is made or how repairable it must be put fixing within reach.

Deep-dive question: When governments reshape what gear people can buy, with EU repairability rules on one side and US import bans and made-in-America pushes on the other, does a remote owner end up with parts and repairs within reach, or only with a different set of gatekeepers?

Riley starts: Rules that force repairability and push production closer to home can pay off for remote owners if they are designed to reach them, and the EU's early gains show the approach can work.
Casey starts: A rule or a launch announcement is not a part on the shelf, and rural owners tend to be the last to see any benefit, so these measures should be judged by what reaches a remote buyer.

  1. The one measure that targets repair directly shows real but incomplete progress: a year on, EU repairability rules have produced some signs of improvement, with open questions about difficulty and cost.
    then, because: If repair rules only partly deliver where they are aimed squarely at repair, what happens when governments act on gear without mentioning repair at all, as the US is doing with import bans?
  2. US moves on foreign gadgets decide what can be bought without saying anything about whether it can be fixed, and that shifts who controls supply.
    then, because: If the bans narrow what can be imported, the fair test is whether a domestic alternative can actually supply the machine and its replacement parts.
  3. The domestic-supply answer is so far one announced model and a long-term goal, which says nothing yet about repair.
    then, because: With one policy aimed at repair and another aimed at origin, the question becomes whether the two have to be joined for a remote owner to benefit.
  4. Repairability and supply security are being handled by separate rules, and a remote owner is only helped where they meet.
    then, because: The test of all this is a household far from a service centre, which is where the Cariboo comes in.
  5. A rural household's real experience of these rules depends on whether parts and fixes reach it, not on where the rule was written.
    then, because: This sets up the landing: whether the repair test should be written into the same rules that decide what gets sold.

Landing: The disagreement is held open: should a rule that restricts or reshapes what gear can be sold also carry a repair requirement for parts and service access, so a remote owner benefits, or should repairability be left to separate law with the risk that supply-security rules leave rural owners with fewer options and no more ability to fix them?
