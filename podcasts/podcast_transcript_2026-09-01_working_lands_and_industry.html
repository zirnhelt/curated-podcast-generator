DEEP DIVE: CARIBOO CONNECTIONS - Working Lands & Industry

Riley: Washington is planning to auction off deep-sea mining leases near the Northern Mariana Islands, close to the Mariana Trench, sometime later this year. This is part of the broader push for domestic sources of critical minerals — cobalt, nickel, manganese, the metals locked up in seabed nodules — so the U.S. isn't stuck depending on Chinese processing capacity for materials it needs for batteries and defense.

Casey: Domestic for whom, though. Guam and the Northern Marianas are the ones objecting the loudest, and they're the ones who'd carry the ecological risk with none of the economic upside landing on their islands.

Riley: That's the actual question worth putting on the table. Does self-sufficient mean the nation as a whole stops depending on a foreign supplier, or does it mean the people who actually live on top of the resource get to decide what happens to it? Right now those two definitions are pulling in opposite directions on the same lease auction.

Casey: Earthjustice and local advocates in the territories have called this an intensified rush for dominance that guarantees an ecological crisis and permanent insecurity for their home islands. That's the phrase they used — permanent insecurity. Not hyperbole from outsiders. That's people describing their own water.

Riley: The underlying vulnerability the U.S. is responding to is real. Most critical-mineral processing capacity sits in China, and that's a genuine strategic exposure, not a manufactured one. But "domestic" is doing something slippery in that sentence. It's borrowing the legitimacy of the mainland's supply-chain anxiety while the actual seabed being auctioned sits off islands that don't get a vote in the Congress making the decision.

Casey: Right. Self-sufficiency for Washington. Permanent dependency, indefinitely, for the Marianas.

Riley: There's a genuine comparison here to what's happening in Brazil. The Supreme Court there — a ruling authored by Justice Flávio Dino, upheld again this month after an earlier decision in March — is letting Cinta Larga communities in Rondônia pursue mineral exploration inside their own territory. Four territories, specifically. But only under conditions: compliance with environmental requirements, and what the ruling calls "major approval among community members."

Casey: That phrase is doing a lot of the actual legal work in that ruling, and it's vague on purpose or vague by accident — hard to tell which. Who decides what counts as major approval? A council vote? A referendum with a quorum requirement? The court didn't specify, and Congress in Brazil still hasn't passed the permanent legislation the ruling explicitly says needs to exist.

Riley: Every functioning consent process starts as an improvised one, though. Impact Benefit Agreements in Canada started exactly this way — courts forcing a negotiation into existence before any legislature had caught up with a framework for it. The vagueness now doesn't mean the standard stays vague forever.

Casey: Except Cinta Larga and other Indigenous leaders have also been rallying in Brasília over land-dispute pressure at the same time this ruling landed. That's not a community unanimously excited about mining its own land. That's real internal division, and the ruling hands a high-stakes decision to communities without giving them the infrastructure — legal counsel, geological expertise, negotiating leverage — to make that decision well.

Riley: Which is where the Yukon story looks different again. Talamore Mining just secured the last major road permit it needed — twenty-nine kilometres of new segments finishing off a two-hundred-and-fourteen-kilometre route connecting its Coffee gold project to Dawson City. And the construction contractor building it, Cobalt Construction, is a Yukon company partnered directly with the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in First Nation.

Casey: Partnered, or subcontracted. There's a real difference between a Nation co-leading a construction project and a Nation being hired onto someone else's permitting timeline with a nicer word attached to the arrangement.

Riley: Talamore's own project materials list four First Nations with stakes in that corridor — Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in, Selkirk First Nation, White River First Nation, and the First Nation of Na-Cho Nyäk Dun — for whom that land carries central cultural importance, not incidental interest.

Casey: And construction on the mine itself hasn't started. There's no Final Investment Decision yet, and mine licenses are still outstanding. The road is the easy part to greenlight because it's infrastructure, not extraction. We don't actually know yet if this is the good version of the story or the same extraction story with better public relations wrapped around it.

Riley: Fair. That's an honest gap.

Casey: Look at the pattern across all three. The U.S. calls seabed mining domestic self-sufficiency while the people on the actual islands get no vote. Brazil calls Cinta Larga approval self-determination while leaving the approval standard for that self-determination completely undefined. Yukon calls a construction partnership a form of consent while the mine that partnership is building toward hasn't even been decided yet. In every single case, "self-sufficient" turns out to be the word used by whoever sits furthest from the actual ground being dug up.

Riley: That's close, but it misses one distinction. The Tŝilhqot'in title case did something none of those three situations has — it gave a Nation actual veto power over development on its own land, upheld by Canada's Supreme Court in a binding ruling, not improvised by a lower court scrambling under political pressure the way the Cinta Larga ruling reads. That's a durable legal network, built over decades of litigation, not a temporary safeguard waiting on legislation that might never come.

Casey: Durable next to Brazil, sure. But a title win doesn't build a Nation's own water-testing lab or its own seismic monitoring station. Xatśūll First Nation is still, a decade past the Mount Polley tailings failure, largely reading data that the mining company itself generates and reports back to them.

Riley: That's actually the sharper version of the question then. It's not whether a Nation has the legal standing to say no — the Tŝilhqot'in ruling settled that question years ago. It's whether a Nation can independently verify what's happening on its own land without depending entirely on the company's own instruments and the company's own reporting schedule.

Casey: Right. Legal self-sufficiency and technical self-sufficiency are two different things, and only one of them shows up in a Supreme Court decision.

Riley: Gibraltar Mine, up near Williams Lake, already runs its own air quality monitoring as a standing line item in its operating budget, not a one-time compliance cost. If that model already exists for air, there's no structural reason water and tailings monitoring couldn't be built the same way — funded permanently into the operating agreement, but run by a party the Nation actually trusts, rather than the company grading its own homework.

Casey: Who runs that independent party is the entire question, though. If it's a consultant the company still writes the cheque for, you've added a layer of process, not a layer of independence. The incentive structure hasn't moved.

Riley: Then the ownership question becomes the literal thing to negotiate — who holds the physical instruments, who owns the raw data, who has the authority to publish results without needing anyone's sign-off first. That's a design choice available right now, on any permit currently being negotiated in this region. It doesn't need new provincial legislation. It needs a different clause written into the next agreement that gets signed.

Casey: A clause is easy to propose at the negotiating table and hard to enforce five years after the ribbon-cutting, once the people who negotiated it have moved on to other jobs and the mine's changed ownership twice through a corporate sale.

Riley: Which is the sharper version of the question again — not whether a Nation or a regional district has independent monitoring capacity today, but whether that capacity, once built, survives a change in ownership the same way a constitutional title right does.

Casey: And nobody in any of these three stories has actually tested that yet. Not the seabed lease, not the Cinta Larga ruling, not the Yukon road.

Riley: So the honest answer is we don't have the case study yet. Not in the Cariboo, not anywhere close.

Casey: We don't. Worth saying plainly instead of pretending one of these three examples is already the model to copy.

Riley: If there's a concrete experiment worth watching for over the next year, it's whether any Nation negotiating a new permit in this region insists on owning the monitoring infrastructure outright — the sensors, the data, the publication rights — rather than accepting consultation as the whole deal and leaving the actual verification to the company's own network.

Casey: That's the structural thing to track. Who owns the sensor, not who signed the agreement photo-op.
