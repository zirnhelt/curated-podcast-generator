Riley: The Northern Miner ran an editorial this week called "Industrial power returns." Its argument is straightforward — for roughly three decades, mining executives had to actively make the case to governments that minerals counted as strategic assets, worth protecting and fast-tracking. Nobody's making that case anymore. Governments are just deciding it's true, on their own, without the industry lobbying for it.

Casey: And deciding something is suddenly "strategic" tends to come with a shortcut attached, historically.

Riley: The shift itself is measurable, not just rhetorical. The U.S. passed the so-called One Big Beautiful Bill Act with streamlined permitting built directly into it. There's a planned fifty percent tariff on copper imports. And the USGS just expanded its official critical minerals list to include metallurgical coal and uranium — categories that didn't used to need that kind of protection because nobody was worried about who controlled the global supply chain for them.

Casey: Every one of those tools is also a bypass mechanism. Streamlined permitting means fewer review cycles, which means fewer chances for a community or a Nation to raise a concern before ground gets broken. A fifty percent tariff protects incumbent North American producers — it doesn't require them to prove the value stays local, or that workers near the mine see any of it.

Riley: The counterfactual is worse, though. Thirty years of treating lithium and copper as ordinary, boring commodities is exactly why North America ended up with essentially zero domestic battery-metal refining capacity, and depends heavily on Chinese processing for materials mined right here. That dependency is the actual, measurable cost of not treating minerals as strategic sooner.

Casey: Look closely at who gets to write the rules once the "strategic" label lands on a project, though. Legal analysts — not industry cheerleaders — are already calling this a genuine paradigm shift, where mining projects get bound up in national security review and international diplomacy instead of ordinary commercial permitting. National security review means fewer questions get asked publicly, not more.

Riley: Fewer questions from Ottawa or Washington, potentially. Not necessarily fewer questions from the Nations whose title actually covers the land in question — that's a different veto point entirely, and it doesn't get streamlined by a federal tariff.

Casey: That distinction is worth actually testing, because right now, in a jurisdiction with weak rule of law, "strategic" mineral status can look exactly like what Honduran farmers are living through. Honduras's own human rights commission puts the death toll in that land conflict at over two hundred people. When land becomes economically strategic without a strong legal framework underneath it, the cost lands on smallholders — not on the corporations behind the label, and not on the government that assigned it.
