Riley: The World Meteorological Organization declared the onset of El Niño in early June. It began strengthening in August, and it's expected to reach record strength by December, with heat and disruptive rain running into 2027. That is a long runway. So when a warning arrives months before the damage, what can a place actually do with the lead time, and what does a global forecast leave each place to work out alone?

Casey: A loud warning isn't a prepared place. The WMO's numbers are global averages. Temperatures from January through September ran about 1.47 degrees above preindustrial levels. An average doesn't tell a town which culvert fails.

Riley: Lead time comes from somewhere, though. Satellite records and seasonal models are why we knew in June what was coming in December. The Niño 3.4 index climbed from about 1.5 degrees above normal in May through July to 2.0 in July. The WHO has classed the expected health effects as an emergency, the first time it has done that for an El Niño. It also published a Grade 3 preparedness plan.

Casey: A plan isn't a result. WMO's September update put the chance that El Niño persists through February 2027 near 100 percent. The record label is the uncertain part. The U.S. Climate Prediction Center gives roughly 60 percent odds on a record-strength season. Sixty percent is a bet you wouldn't take with a highway.

Riley: Sixty percent is still a forecast you can plan around. You don't wait for certainty to fund a drainage fix. You size it for the range.

Casey: Sized for which end of the range? The WMO's secretary-general called the situation "off the charts." A culvert needs a diameter, and a diameter needs a number.

Riley: Then take the numbers that exist. Nairobi has one. Bramwell Simiyu, the disaster-management chief for Nairobi County, says the drainage system was designed 30 years ago for 2.5 million people. It only changes how soon someone has to start paying it.

Casey: A warning doesn't build a sewer. Months of lead time won't turn $192 million into a line in a budget that survives the next election.

Riley: Sewers aren't a warning's job, though. Ghana's gap is cheaper. Casey: Protocols are cheap to write and expensive to keep running. Who staffs the heat alert in year five? If the answer is a volunteer with a binder, the protocol dies in the first quiet season.

Riley: Then put it inside an existing health office. Make the heat alert a line in someone's job description, and rehearse it before the season, not during it. The cost is staff hours, and those are the cheapest part of this.

Casey: Cheap to say. Who checks the alert reaches the clinic on the far side of the river? Notices fail most often where nobody checked who was on the list.
