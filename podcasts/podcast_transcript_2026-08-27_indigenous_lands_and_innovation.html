Riley: Here's the question worth putting on the table directly. When a language is coming back from the edge of loss, who gets to decide whether the person teaching it counts as qualified?

Casey: The elder who's spoken it since childhood, or the person with a linguistics degree who's never heard it spoken outside a recording booth.

Riley: Right. And B.C.'s public education system has an actual, specific answer to that, which is uncomfortable in places. Most provinces require a full teaching certificate through years of coursework administered by a provincial regulatory body. B.C. maintains an alternate certification pathway specifically for fluent Indigenous language speakers to enter a classroom without going through that standard route.

Casey: Does have, or is piloting?

Riley: As it's publicly described, it's a standing alternate pathway, not a pilot program. That distinction matters. It's an actual acknowledgment, in policy, that fluency itself functions as a credential.

Casey: It's a narrow acknowledgment though. It gets one person into one public school classroom under one certification stream. It doesn't touch who certifies the curriculum that person teaches from, who decides which spelling system a textbook uses, or who a funding body recognizes as a qualified linguistic authority when a grant application asks for professional sign-off.

Riley: That's the layer underneath the layer.

Casey: It is. Take Secwepemctsín specifically. There's a standardized writing system that came out of academic partnerships going back decades — it lets you build consistent teaching materials across scattered communities. But elders in different Secwépemc communities pronounce words differently depending on regional dialect. Canim Lake isn't Alkali Lake isn't Adams Lake.

Riley: So the written standard can end up correcting a living speaker instead of describing them.

Casey: That's the risk. A grandmother's actual, lived pronunciation gets treated as a deviation from "the" official form, when the official form was one linguist's snapshot of one community's speech at one point in time, decades ago.

Riley: Where I'd push back a little is that the same academic partnership is what produced any written standard at all. Without it, there's no textbook, no consistent teaching material, nothing durable to hand a kid in Williams Lake who isn't sitting with an elder every single day of the week. FirstVoices works because the Nations using it hold the actual keys — they decide what gets recorded, who reviews entries, what stays restricted from public view. That's governance built into the platform from the start, not an academic import layered on top after the fact.

Casey: FirstVoices works because a Nation controls the platform itself. The open question is whether that same control extends up into the credentialing pipeline that decides who gets paid a salary to teach the language inside a public school building. Different institution, same underlying shape.

Riley: Fair to separate those two things. Though I'd argue the second is downstream of the first. If a Nation runs its own fluency documentation and recording infrastructure long enough, it builds an evidence base that a school district eventually has to reckon with, on the Nation's terms rather than an outside linguist's.
