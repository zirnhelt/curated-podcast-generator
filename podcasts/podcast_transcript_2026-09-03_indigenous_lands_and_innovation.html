DEEP DIVE: CARIBOO CONNECTIONS - Indigenous Lands & Innovation

Riley: There's a story out this week about a clean-energy project going into an old West Virginia strip mine. Google-backed, pairing solar with a new zinc battery chemistry from a company called Eos, on five hundred acres that have sat idle for twenty years. Reads well on paper — coal scar becomes clean power for the grid.

Casey: It's a good decarbonization story. It is not an Indigenous story. Nothing in the reporting ties it to tribal land, tribal partnership, or tribal decision-making of any kind. It's a utility and a tech company doing a corporate retrofit on land nobody's disputing ownership of anymore.

Riley: Which is the question worth asking this week. Is the win here self-sufficiency, or is it who's plugged into the right network? That West Virginia site only exists because Google needed grid capacity nearby and the utility needed dispatchable storage close to where demand already sits. It's a connected project wearing the costume of land reclamation.

Casey: And that's the trap in calling every old-extraction-site retrofit an innovation story. Reused land isn't the same as land under Indigenous control. If we're talking about energy sovereignty today, the sharper test case isn't a former coal lease in Appalachia.

Riley: It's the tribal-led projects. NPR reported this year on a utility-scale solar-plus-battery installation brought online on tribal land in New Mexico. Native Renewables has been running off-grid solar installs for Navajo and Hopi households that never had grid power to begin with — not a retrofit, a first connection, built and owned on the Nation's own terms from the start.

Casey: Those are different animals. One's a Nation deciding what gets built on land it already controls. The other's a former coal company's lease getting a second life because Google needed storage capacity somewhere, and West Virginia happened to have the acreage and the interconnect already in place.

Riley: So — are those Navajo and Hopi off-grid systems self-sufficient, or connected in a different sense? Connected to Native Renewables' financing, connected to solar hardware manufactured somewhere else.

Casey: Both. A household owning the panels on its own roof is self-sufficient in the way that matters most day to day — nobody outside the community can flip the switch off or dictate the terms of use. But the hardware still comes out of a global supply chain. Nobody's manufacturing solar cells on the reservation.

Riley: And nobody's mining silicon out near Hanceville either. Self-sufficiency was never about building every component from scratch. It's about who holds the decision that matters — whether the power stays on, and who has the authority to turn it off.

Casey: That framing has its own risk, though. Programs like Native Renewables run largely on grant cycles and philanthropic funding, as publicly reported. If that funding tightens, the panels stay bolted to the roof, but the maintenance contracts and replacement parts don't necessarily keep following.

Riley: That's a real constraint, but a different failure mode than the alternative. A funding gap is a problem you solve by finding a different funder. A grid that was never built out to those homes at all is a problem you can't solve by writing a check — someone still has to run the line.

Casey: Fine. But look at the flip side, because it's the sharper counterexample to any story claiming clean innovation automatically helps land-connected communities. The Guardian reported this week that Barclays faces a formal complaint over its financing of the Rampal coal plant in Bangladesh, built right next to the Sundarbans — the largest mangrove forest on Earth.

Riley: Two million people depend on that forest for fisheries and forestry work.

Casey: And the complaint alleges the plant's pollution and erosion risks threaten exactly that livelihood base. This is what happens when the capital and the decision-making sit somewhere else — a bank office in London. The people whose land and water are actually on the line don't get a vote on whether that financing goes through.

Riley: Which is the strongest argument for the New Mexico and Navajo models, not against them. When a Nation owns the asset outright, a bank in a different hemisphere can't unilaterally decide a project's worth the risk to somebody else's mangrove forest. Rampal is what happens when "connected" means connected to capital instead of connected to the land the project sits on.

Casey: Don't let that tip into "Indigenous-owned, therefore automatically safe." Ownership changes who's accountable when something goes wrong. It doesn't eliminate the tension between a project's economics and its environmental footprint. A tribally owned solar farm can still sign a bad interconnection deal that reads like the Rampal financing reads here — outside capital writing the terms because the Nation needs that capital to move at all.

Riley: That's the real leverage question, then. Not self-sufficient versus connected as a binary — which kind of connection, and who wrote the contract.

Casey: Right. And that's where the Cariboo piece of this stops being theoretical. It loops straight back to the hatchery story from this hour — a permanent conservation hatchery going in near Hanceville, on Tŝilhqot'in territory, rebuilding salmon runs that drought and habitat loss have hammered for years.

Riley: The published reporting doesn't say who holds decision-making authority over brood stock selection, release targets, or how the run-strength data gets shared once it's collected. Not a knock on the project itself. A permanent facility instead of a temporary one is real progress — but permanent for whom, and accountable to whom, are two separate questions the article leaves open.

Casey: If the Nation holds a seat on brood stock decisions and gets the run-strength data directly, rather than through a provincial summary released six months later, that's closer to the Navajo solar model. If it's a provincial or federal facility that happens to sit on Tŝilhqot'in land and reports back on its own schedule, that's closer to Rampal with better intentions attached.

Riley: We don't have enough in what's published to know which one this is.

Casey: There's a version of this worth trying that doesn't require waiting on that answer, though. If a Cariboo Nation wanted to test the ownership question on a smaller scale than a hatchery, a community-scale solar-plus-storage pilot on band-owned land — sized for a school or a health centre, not a whole grid — would answer the "who decides" question inside months, not years.

Riley: That's the kind of thing BC Hydro's community and Indigenous partnership programs claim to fund, at least on paper. The real test isn't the grant application. It's whether the interconnection agreement lets the Nation walk away from BC Hydro's terms later, or whether it locks them into a twenty-year contract written entirely by someone else's lawyers, in print small enough to need a magnifying glass.

Casey: Which is worth reading line by line before signing, and worth raising at a band council meeting before a project like that gets announced, not after the ribbon-cutting.

Riley: There's a data-centre echo here too, since it showed up twice today. The G20 protests over AI data centre expansion are about the same underlying question — communities objecting to water and power draw from projects they had no say in siting. A rural Nation weighing a data centre lease on its land would be negotiating from the same position Bangladesh's fisheries communities were never given: a seat at the table before the contract's signed, not a complaint process after construction starts.

Casey: So — self-sufficient or well-connected. It doesn't resolve cleanly, and maybe it isn't supposed to. West Virginia is connected and calls itself clean. New Mexico is connected too, just to a different network — one the Nation can actually renegotiate.
