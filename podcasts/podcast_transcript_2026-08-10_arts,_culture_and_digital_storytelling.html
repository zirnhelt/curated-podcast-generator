Casey: The Atlantic ran a piece this week on orchestral conducting — framed as one of the most widely misunderstood professions in music. A conductor doesn't produce a single sound themselves. They're standing there interpreting the score, shaping timing, dynamics, the hundred tiny decisions that an audience experiences as the orchestra's collective choice, not one person's judgment call.

Riley: The invisible-labor angle is the interesting part of that piece. Everybody in the audience sees the orchestra playing. Almost nobody sees the person making the actual editorial decisions that make the whole thing cohere into something worth listening to.

Casey: Right — and that's essentially the editorial function in local journalism, except almost nobody in a town this size gets paid to do that job anymore. Nobody's conducting.

Riley: That's actually the real question worth sitting with today. Not whether rural communities can produce more raw content — they clearly can, right now, cheaply, on a phone in someone's pocket. The question is whether anyone in the chain is doing the conducting. Verification. Following up on a claim before it runs. Knowing which sources to trust and which need a second call. Deciding what doesn't get published because it isn't confirmed yet.

Casey: The Williams Lake Tribune still does some real version of that work — actual reporters checking actual facts before anything goes to print. That's not nothing, and it shouldn't get waved away. But newsroom staffing at papers that size has been shrinking steadily for over a decade, and a lot of what fills the resulting gap now is unedited social posts and one-person podcasts with no fact-checker and no editor pushing back before publication.

Riley: Podcasting genuinely does lower the barrier to entry, though, and that's not a small thing. Anyone with a laptop and forty minutes of spare time can publish something that reaches an audience a legacy outlet in this region never could reach — no printing costs, no delivery trucks, no ad-sales department eating half the revenue before a single story gets written. That matters in a region where the nearest big-market outlet doesn't send anyone to cover a council meeting in 100 Mile House.

Casey: A lower barrier to publishing isn't the same as a lower barrier to trustworthy publishing. A newspaper's credibility historically came from a whole chain of accountability behind it — an editor who could actually be fired for running something false, a corrections column that ran in the same physical paper as the original error, a lawyer on retainer if a story got contested. A podcast host doesn't inherit any of that structure automatically. They have to build every piece of it themselves, deliberately, and most simply don't.

Riley: Some do, though — and it's a choice, not an inherent limitation baked into the format itself. There are independent journalists who've built exactly that kind of accountability into a newsletter or podcast — a public track record people can check, an explicit correction policy, transparency about who they talked to and who declined to comment. It takes deliberate effort, but the tool doesn't prevent it.

Casey: It's a choice that requires someone to actually make it, and then keep making it, consistently, without an institution enforcing it from above. That's the actual gap. Tools don't create editorial discipline on their own. People do — and historically, mostly only when something concrete depends on it. A job. A reputation. A paycheck tied directly to getting it right.
