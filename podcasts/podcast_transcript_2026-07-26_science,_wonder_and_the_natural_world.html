Casey: Let's start with the lifespan story, because ScienceAlert flagged something specific — researchers are narrowing down which drugs already sitting in pharmacies might actually slow biological aging. Metformin, a decades-old diabetes drug. Rapamycin, an immunosuppressant originally developed to stop organ transplant rejection. Not new biotech. Old molecules, new questions being asked of them.

Riley: And this is the part that gets genuinely exciting. Rapamycin extended maximum lifespan in mice by roughly 14 percent in females, 9 percent in males, in NIA-funded studies. Metformin shows reduced biological-aging markers in cell and molecular clock studies. These are drugs people already take, with known safety profiles, established manufacturing, and no exotic gene therapy or six-figure biotech pipeline required. Molecules that have been in the pharmacy since before either of us existed as a concept.

Casey: Mouse lifespan isn't human lifespan. The actual human trial — TAME, Targeting Aging with Metformin — has been in planning for years, and it's a genuinely long slog. It's a Phase 3 study in non-diabetic adults 65 to 79, tracking a composite outcome across cardiovascular disease, cancer, dementia, and death. There's a preprint circulating that cites something like a 14 percent reduction in that composite at 18 months, but that's in a small subset of participants. That is not the same as a finished, peer-reviewed, full-cohort result.

Riley: Sure, it's early. But early and wrong aren't the same thing. The mechanism is conserved across an enormous range of species — mTOR inhibition slows aging in yeast, in worms, in flies, in mice. That's not coincidence, that's a shared biological pathway going back a very long way on the evolutionary tree, which is exactly the kind of signal that usually holds up under scrutiny.

Casey: Here's the harder problem, though, and it's not biology — it's money. There's a real argument going around, and it's not a fringe one, that even if these drugs work, no pharmaceutical company will ever run the definitive trial to prove it at scale, because you can't patent metformin. Phase 3 trials cost hundreds of millions of dollars. Nobody recoups that cost proving a fifty-year-old generic drug works better than expected.

Riley: That's an incentive problem, not a science problem.

Casey: It's still a problem that determines whether the science ever actually gets confirmed for the rest of us. "Scientists are narrowing them down" is a nicer headline than "and there's no commercial entity left standing that has any reason to pay for the trial that would prove it conclusively."

Riley: Which is actually where this connects to something wilder — the flip side of trying to slow human aging down is looking at a species that's evolved to speed its own aging up on purpose. Pacific salmon.

Casey: The post-spawn die-off.

Riley: Right. Sockeye, chinook, coho — they swim upriver, spawn once, and then their bodies essentially disintegrate within days of finishing. Cortisol floods their system, their organs shut down in a specific sequence, and it happens on a schedule tight enough that biologists genuinely call it programmed senescence. It's not gradual decline the way we think of aging. It's a switch that flips, deliberately, on a biological timer.

Casey: So on one end you've got researchers hunting for a pill to push human aging further out, and on the other end, a fish that evolution wired to age itself to death on a deadline — because a fast, total collapse after spawning returns more nutrients to the river system, feeding everything from bears to insect larvae to the next generation of fry, than a slow decline ever would.

Riley: That's the wonder of it, honestly. Aging isn't one universal process running at different speeds across species. It's a dial that evolution turns differently depending on what actually benefits the organism's strategy — slow it way down if you're a long-lived mammal investing in offspring over years and years, or crank it to maximum if you're a salmon whose entire genetic contribution to the world ends the moment eggs hit gravel.

Casey: Which raises a real question about what "slowing aging" even means once you're comparing across species that different. Rapamycin's mechanism, mTOR signaling, actually plays a documented role in salmon senescence too — it's tied into the same nutrient-sensing pathway that triggers their collapse after spawning. So the exact same molecular lever that might buy a human a few extra healthy years is, in a fish, wired directly to a self-destruct sequence.

Riley: Exactly — same gene family, completely opposite evolutionary job description. That's not a contradiction, that's just how deeply conserved pathways tend to work across the tree of life. The switch itself is ancient. What flips it, and in which direction, and how fast, depends entirely on what the organism's whole life strategy needs it to do.

Casey: It's a genuinely good teaching hook, honestly — that's basically what Scout Island's Salmon in the Classroom program is showing kids without necessarily naming the mechanism out loud.
