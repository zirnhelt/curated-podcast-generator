Riley: When a community invests in something for its kids now, what obligation does that create toward the people who inherit it twenty years down the line? Not as an abstraction — as a real design choice happening right now at Xatśūll First Nation.

Casey: The acting workshop.

Riley: The acting workshop, but not just the acting workshop. Xatśūll ran a three-day acting program for its children and youth, and it's easy to file that as a nice, small cultural story and move on. But Xatśūll also runs Xatsull Development, an economic development arm explicitly built to leverage the Nation's territorial rights and title into sustainable businesses. And in 2020, the province purchased ranch lands for Xatśūll as part of ongoing treaty negotiations. Land base, business development, youth arts program — all inside the same Nation, in the same stretch of years.

Casey: Or it's three separate line items that happen to belong to the same Nation, and we're drawing the throughline ourselves.

Riley: Look at the sequencing. Land restitution first, business infrastructure second, cultural programming third. That's what capacity-building looks like when it works: you get the land base, you build revenue off it, the revenue funds the next generation's leadership pipeline. The workshop isn't decoration — it's downstream of the ranch purchase.

Casey: Downstream by how much, though? A three-day workshop and one ranch purchase in six years is a thin thread. Treaty negotiations in this province move at a pace measured in decades — the Tŝilhqot'in title case took from 1989 to 2014 to reach the Supreme Court, and that's considered fast. A ranch parcel and a youth program can just as easily be the "soft" wins governments and Nations both point to while the actual jurisdiction — resource rights, subsurface title, who signs off on a mine permit — stays exactly where it's always been.

Riley: The ArchDaily piece on craft construction economies is useful here, even though it's about rammed earth and bamboo in the Global South, not Indigenous land. The argument is that celebrating handmade materials in magazines and awards means nothing if the labor economy underneath — training pipelines, material supply, artisan wages — stays fragile and undercapitalized. Culture gets aestheticized while the infrastructure underneath it starves.

Casey: Which is exactly my worry about the workshop, stated by someone writing about architecture in Burundi.

Riley: (chuckles) Except read the critique the other way. It isn't "stop doing craft programs" — it's "craft only becomes durable when you control the material and labor systems around it, not just the aesthetic output." That's precisely what Xatsull Development describes itself as building — control over the land, the business, the revenue stream — with the workshop as one visible output of a system, not a substitute for one.

Casey: Owning the underlying business arm is a different animal than a Nation simply receiving cultural programming money from a provincial ministry with no land component attached. If Xatsull Development is actually generating independent revenue rather than running on annual grant cycles, that's a structurally different position. But do we know that it is?
