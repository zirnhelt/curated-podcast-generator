Riley: A B.C. plywood manufacturer told Global News last week it cannot absorb the new fifty-percent U.S. tariff on plywood, paper, fibreboard, particleboard, and veneered wood panels. That tariff took effect around August 19th, stacked on top of existing softwood lumber duties. CIBC analysts point out raw softwood lumber is exempt from this particular round — it's the finished panel products taking the hit. At Richmond Plywood, the tariff threatens roughly a fifth of total sales, concentrated in the high-end products where the margin actually lives.

Casey: So the mill can retool, get more efficient, invest in better kiln technology, do everything the innovation crowd tells it to do — and none of it matters if Washington shuts the door.

Riley: That's the part the modernization talk skips. This round of tariffs is expected to touch roughly twenty-eight billion dollars in Canadian exports. A mill can improve its grading systems and its process efficiency and still lose the whole play because of a decision made in a country where nobody on that floor has a vote.

Casey: So who's allowed to fix a problem like this, and who just has to absorb it? A mill worker in Quesnel gets no say on tariff policy. Neither does the owner, most of the time, however well-run the operation is.

Riley: And the leverage that does exist isn't distributed evenly. B.C.'s premier has declined to make concessions — putting U.S. alcohol back on shelves, for one — without reciprocal tariff relief for forestry.
