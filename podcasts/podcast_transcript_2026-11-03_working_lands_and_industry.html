Casey: The northern B.C. tanker ban is still in place. The House voted at second reading on Wednesday, October 28th, and the Conservative bill to repeal it, Bill C-264, failed. The vote split along party lines, and only the Conservatives voted to send it to committee.

Riley: Right. The NDP and the Bloc voted with the Liberals, so this wasn't one party holding the line. The mechanism is simple. The law bars tankers carrying more than 12,500 metric tonnes of crude from loading or unloading in the covered northern waters. Smaller shipments for community resupply still move. What it limits is how much goes on any one ship.

Casey: So does the cap block the exports, the jobs and the prices? Or does it only decide where the cargo goes, which route carries the risk, and who gets paid for carrying it?

Riley: The arithmetic answers part of that. Strathcona Resources wants to move up to 250,000 barrels a day of Western Canadian heavy crude, by rail on existing tracks to a new terminal near Prince Rupert, then by tanker to Asian markets. That's roughly 40 million litres a day.

Casey: And a single ship under the ban tops out at 12,500 tonnes. Run typical heavy crude density through it and 40 million litres is something like 37,000 tonnes. Call it three capped loads a day, as a rough back-of-envelope.

Riley: Three loads a day is a scheduling problem. You don't remove the volume. You change the berths, the timing and the number of ships.

Casey: More ships on the same coast. Every load is another trip through the waters the ban covers.

Riley: Or the loading moves out of the covered waters entirely. That's what the pipeline discussion is about, and it's the piece I'd look at first.

Casey: Mm-hm. We'll get there. First the price argument.

Riley: Repeal supporters say the ban stands between Canadian resources and better prices, and that Canada can't be an energy superpower with federal policy in the way. Danielle Smith backed the repeal. Conservative natural resources critic Carol Anstey argues a private shipping proposal means Canadian jobs and more export customers. Those are the claims a critic has to answer.

Casey: Both are projections from the people selling it. Until someone puts a per-barrel figure on "better price" and says who keeps the difference, it's a slogan.

Riley: Who keeps it matters. But a producer that can reach a second set of buyers in Asia is making a concrete claim. Diversifying where the barrels go changes what a barrel is worth to the seller. You can test that.

Casey: Test it with what? The gap between a coastal sale and a landlocked sale is the number nobody puts in a press release. Show me the discount and the word superpower starts to mean something.
