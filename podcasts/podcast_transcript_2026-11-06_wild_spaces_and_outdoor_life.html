Riley: When a sensor sees the fire or the bear first, who is on the hook for the next move, and does the tool change what people do out there? The Italian fire we mentioned up top is the case for sensors. The detection worked, fast. The crew still had trouble reaching the ground.

Casey: The sensor worked. The terrain didn't. Nobody budgets for that gap.

Riley: The detection capability is real. The Forbes piece describes systems that forecast fire behaviour and classify species from sensor data, and that is a large step past a lookout tower. But a minute saved at the sensor helps the crew only if the access road is open.

Casey: Minutes saved at the sensor, then hours lost on a rutted road. Who maintains that road, and who decides which fire gets the crew first when two sensors go off in the same afternoon?

Riley: That's a dispatch problem, and it has an answer: a ranked queue, written down before the season starts. Detection is what makes the queue possible in the first place.

Casey: Writing it down is easy. The queue is only as good as the crew that answers it, and crews are the line item that gets cut first.

Riley: Then ask for the staffing number next to the sensor number. Public budgets carry both.

Casey: San Bernardino is the same question with drones attached. Five hundred gallons of foam per drone, and that 10-square-foot goal. Nothing says confidence like a number that small. Who launches them, who keeps them flying in January, and what do they cost per year once the demo is over?

Riley: The county hasn't released a purchase price or an annual fee for its five drones. The closest comparison is the Aspen Fire Protection District, which signed a $5 million multi-year agreement for a similar five-drone system. And drones skip the road, which is the whole pitch. The first hour is where most fires are won or lost.

Riley: A published target is how a track record starts. The county can be held to the 90 percent.

Riley: Mount Shasta is the failure on the other side. Three 20-year-olds had a planning tool and still summited at 7:30 pm with too little food. Software can print a turnaround time at the trailhead.

Casey: Printing it was never the issue. Nobody turned them around in the afternoon, and Meyers's point was that they stopped doing that job themselves. A chatbot can't see the sky from the rock face.

Riley: Then build the check into the tool. A planning app that refuses to hand over a route until you enter a turnaround time is a design fix.

Casey: And who keeps that check current across every trail? Snow changes a route in a week. The app doesn't know the snow is there.

Riley: Which is why the off-grid layer matters. That Colorado town where a flood took out the fibre and the phones went with it is the argument for a Meshtastic node, which sends a short text over radio without a tower. The build guide shows the hobbyist version: a board, a battery, a small panel and a weatherproof case. For a crew on a backcountry road, that's the difference between a message and silence.

Casey: The author himself doubts the panel keeps the battery charged. Off-grid kit works until the first cold week.

Riley: So test it in a cold week and log the voltage. A dead node during a test teaches you something. A dead node during a search costs you.

Casey: Then who reads the log?

Riley: Montana gives the people side of the same problem. Fifteen severely food-conditioned bears did not gather overnight. A camera with a motion log on that Bozeman property might have caught the feeding in its early weeks, before the bears became a euthanasia case.

Casey: Officials still don't know how long the feeding went on. A camera records what's in front of it. Somebody has to drive out and tell the people doing the feeding to stop.

The first work was all about people: searchers out, the area secured, and the two of them brought home.

Casey: And once that's done, the question shifts to the bear. Or bears. The Conservation Officer Service said more than one grizzly may have been involved. Insp. Riley: As of the Wednesday update, no bears had been trapped, though there were multiple sightings. Casey: A DNA match is a better standard than a sighting. It still depends on what was collected at the scene, and on catching the right animal at all. Last year's bear walked away from every trap. If more than one grizzly was there, a match on one bear tells you nothing about the other.

Riley: The province's count matters for proportion. CBC's figures show two fatal grizzly attacks in B.C. from 2012 to 2025, in 2017 and 2025, against 64 recorded grizzly attacks in the same period.
