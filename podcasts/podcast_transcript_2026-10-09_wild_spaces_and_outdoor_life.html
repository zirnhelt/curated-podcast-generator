Riley: Simon Fraser University's model puts the cost of climate-driven Canadian wildfire at more than $2 trillion over the 21st century. The study is by Sian Kou-Giesbrecht and was published in Environmental Research. SFU News reported it on October 7th. Under a realistic worst case of three to four degrees of warming, the model puts total annual costs at about $43 billion by the end of the century. Kou-Giesbrecht says the researchers wanted to translate the issue into terms the public can grasp. A dollar figure is something a council can set against a budget, and that's a use I'd defend.

Casey: Look at what the dollar figure counts. Wildfire suppression. Net timber yield loss. Nature-based recreation loss. Plus the global damage from the carbon the fires release. That's the whole domestic ledger, and it's three lines wide.

Riley: Those are three real costs. Suppression is what governments spend. Timber is what the forest was worth to a mill. Recreation is what people lose when a trail closes for a season. Each line has a method behind it.

Casey: It's real as far as it goes. The study says the numbers are probably conservative, because it leaves out Canadian wildfire health impacts, evacuations, infrastructure loss, and economic disruption. It also leaves out singular extreme events, like the 2023 season, which forced more than 200 communities to evacuate.

Riley: Conservative means it's a floor, and a floor is still a number you can plan around. If the model is light, the fix is to add lines, not to throw out the frame.

Casey: A floor that leaves out evacuations is the number a town hears when it's already been evacuated. Nobody in the model is paying for the hotel room, the lost wages, or the week a family spent without knowing whether the house was still standing.

Riley: The study also says that if warming stays at or below two degrees, costs look similar to recent decades. That's a planning target with a price tag attached. The authors' point is that the trajectory is still set by present decisions, which is a reason to act, not a reason to give up.

Casey: Two degrees is a target. What I want to know is what the model puts on recreation loss, in dollars, for a trail that burned, and whether that number means anything for a trail a family rides every August.

Riley: Recreation values are usually estimated from travel costs or visitor surveys, asking what people would pay to keep a place open. It's imperfect, but it isn't a guess.

Casey: And that method values the trail from the outside. Someone who runs a horse outfitting business on that trail is in the estimate as a line item, if they're in it at all. The season they lost is a single number, and it doesn't say whether they came back the next year.

Riley: Then the question is whether a model that's wrong about the people is more wrong than a hectare total that's wrong about the forest.

Casey: So what is a wildfire total actually measuring? Hectares burned tell you how much ground was in fire. They don't tell you whether the forest comes back in a decade, whether the grass returns for cattle, or whether a trail reopens with the same footing. A hectare counts area. It doesn't count recovery.

Riley: Agreed on the hectare. It's the figure the headlines use because it's the one you can measure from a plane.

Casey: Which is why it's the one we keep using.

Riley: Right. And a plane can also count how many hectares came back green by the next spring. That's a second measurement, and the province could publish both.

Casey: Publish both, then tell me who reads the second one. The recovery number tends to disappear from the news cycle before the land has finished recovering.

Casey: Then take the smoke. NPR's story on smoke ecology centres on a researcher named Sanderfoot, who is trying to make the study of smoke's effects on wild animals a field of its own. Her earlier work includes a 2021 study on how wildfire smoke affects bird detection in Washington State. A 2024 study in The Auk analyzed two decades of banding data in California. Acute exposure to fine particles reduced bird capture rates. Chronic exposure had a positive effect on capture rates across the species studied.

Riley: That's the part that matters for measurement. Banding data is one of the longest-running monitoring tools we have. A two-decade dataset in one state lets scientists see patterns a single fire season would hide. The field is maturing because the data is piling up, and the authors didn't stop at one dataset.

Casey: Piling up, sure. But look at what the number is. A capture rate is how many birds went into a net on a given morning. It isn't bird health. The Auk paper also found that body condition was affected. And then the chronic result runs the wrong way: more smoke over the long run, more birds caught.

Riley: That's a counterintuitive result, and counterintuitive results are where a field learns something. You don't throw out the dataset because one finding surprised people.

Casey: No. But you don't build a prevention program on a sign that flips depending on the time window. When the proxy gives the opposite answer over a different timescale, the proxy is what you have to question.

Riley: The fix is to measure more and measure longer. Monitoring networks are how you find out whether the proxy is misleading you.

Casey: Fine. Then the networks need to track what the proxy misses. Birds caught, yes. Birds that never came back to the burned slope, no. Nobody's banding net catches an absence.

Riley: Catrin Edgeley, at Utah State University, makes a related point in an editorial for the International Journal of Wildland Fire. Community wildfire adaptation may underperform because it downplays local context and local input. Her argument draws on community-based research across the U.S. She and Burnett made a similar case in 2025 about rural smoke adaptation: approaches have to match the social conditions of each community. A 2024 paper in the journal Fire questions whether standard community archetypes are still useful for wildfire planning at all.

Casey: That's the same argument that has been made about every template for a long time. A standard smoke forecast is useful. A standard plan for a rural town can be wrong about what the town depends on, and the town is the one that finds out.

Riley: Templates give small communities something to start from. Most small councils don't have a planner on staff. A proven framework and a standard forecast save months of work, and months are what a council doesn't have during fire season.

Casey: And the rancher who runs cattle on that grassland knows which fields recover and which turn to weeds in a bad year. The template doesn't ask. Edgeley's point is that the failure of the template is the missing ask. The people who live with the cost are the ones it skips.

Riley: Then build the ask into the template. Standard tools, with local input as a required field. The tool and the consultation aren't in competition.

Casey: Required field is a tidy phrase. It still has to be filled in by someone who gets listened to, and that usually happens after the fire, when the decision is already made.

Riley: Which is where the cost lands. The model excludes evacuations, but the cost of an evacuation is real to a family in Quesnel or Williams Lake even if the model doesn't price it. The BCAA-backed mental health program in Williams Lake is a cost that appears in no national total.

Casey: Good example. That program exists because someone is carrying the cost after the flames are out. The total counts the fire. It doesn't count the people in the months after.

Riley: And the Quesnel food hub fire is a loss of shared infrastructure, the kind of thing a national model would fold into infrastructure loss, which it also excludes. Local infrastructure gets rebuilt by local people with local budgets, and the rebuild shows up nowhere except the local paper.

Casey: So who carries the cost of a bad year? The province carries suppression. Insurers carry some of the structures, and policyholders carry the premiums after. The trail society carries the closed trail. The rancher carries the grazing that doesn't come back. The model books the total and loses the names.

Riley: Then the practical answer is for communities to keep their own record. A loss register, per fire, kept by the regional district or the local government. Trail closures counted in days. Grazing lost in animal units. Facilities closed. Households displaced. And the date each one came back.
