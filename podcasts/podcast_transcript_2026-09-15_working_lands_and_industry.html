DEEP DIVE: CARIBOO CONNECTIONS - Working Lands & Industry

Riley: There's a question sitting underneath a lot of the agriculture news this week. When a government or a market decides an operation, a herd, a forest is worth saving, who's actually in the room making that call, and who's just living with the paperwork afterward?

Casey: The USDA gave us a live test case for exactly that. Secretary Brooke Rollins announced the Ranchers First Initiative — five actions meant to help rebuild the U.S. cattle herd, which is sitting at its lowest level in 75 years.

Riley: And it's not a press release the industry is ignoring. R-CALF USA — a rancher advocacy group, not a government mouthpiece — called it "important steps" against what they describe as policy-driven forces suppressing marketplace competition and enabling foreign capture of the beef supply chain.

Casey: Cautiously. Notice the hedge — "important steps," not "the fix." Producers quoted in RealAgriculture are a lot less generous. One rancher flat out called it "a nice fluff piece."

Riley: Why fluff?

Casey: Because the expanded heifer retention insurance still costs the rancher more money out of pocket every year, and the FSA loan requirements ask for cash flow proformas that don't match how a cattle operation's income actually lands through a calendar year. That's paperwork written like a tech startup's pitch deck, not a cattle ranch's ledger.

Riley: Sure, nothing says "understands a drought summer" like a spreadsheet built for quarterly earnings calls. But that's a design flaw in the form, not proof the whole initiative is empty — R-CALF's endorsement means the underlying diagnosis is right, even if the loan paperwork itself needs fixing.

Casey: Being right about the disease and being useless about the treatment are two different failures. The second one is the one that shows up on a rancher's balance sheet come renewal season, not the first.

Riley: Cariboo ranching runs a version of the same math — commodity prices, drought, and now support programs designed somewhere else entirely. Whoever writes the eligibility criteria decides who qualifies and who doesn't.

Casey: And it almost never gets debated out loud. Someone in a federal office designs the form, and it becomes the gate everyone downstream has to pass through, whether it fits their operation or not.

Riley: Which is exactly why the Columbia Basin story sits well next to this one. Columbia Basin Trust is running two programs right now helping food producers install solar and trial new crops — reducing input costs and diversifying what actually grows there as weather patterns shift.

Casey: That's a regional trust with local producers represented, not a federal agency writing loan criteria from an office two thousand miles from the nearest cow.

Riley: A trust board with people who actually run operations in that basin designs support that fits the operations underneath it. A federal loan office designs support that fits its own audit requirements, and the rancher has to make the two things match.

Casey: Which brings up the Bolivia story, because it's the sharper, higher-stakes version of the same problem. In Pando region, in the northern Bolivian Amazon, a 2024 court ruling opened the door to carbon credit deals on Indigenous land, and now companies are racing to sign contracts with communities before those communities fully understand what they're valuing, or what rights they might be signing away.

Riley: A Mongabay investigation from last November documented one company, called Biota, locking in multiple carbon contracts plus a letter of intent with local nonprofits and a university, all in the same region, moving fast, ahead of any community-wide understanding of the terms.

Casey: Leaders in Pando are publicly calling it a new form of extraction. Instead of monetizing timber or land the way earlier booms did, someone's now monetizing the air above the forest.

Riley: Next someone will figure out how to put a price on the view.

Casey: Don't give them ideas.

Riley: It's not proof carbon markets themselves are a bad idea, though. It's a governance failure — thin legal capacity in the region, brokers moving faster than anyone can get a lawyer to actually read the contract. The fact that Pando's leaders are publicly denouncing these deals is itself a sign the 2001 collective title recognition still functions as a check. In an old-style extractive boom, there'd be no legal standing to object to anything at all.

Casey: Having the legal standing to object after a deal's already signed isn't the same as having the leverage to negotiate before it. The contracts with Biota are already inked. Objection after the fact doesn't unwind the terms.

Riley: That's an argument for building legal and technical capacity faster in these communities, not for treating carbon markets as inherently rigged against Indigenous landholders.

Casey: It's an argument for asking who gets to write the valuation methodology before the money shows up at the village plaza. Nobody in Pando was in that room when the price of a ton of carbon got set — the identical problem to the FSA loan proformas, just with a longer legal history behind it, and higher stakes riding on the outcome.

Riley: Which is exactly where Central Chilcotin Rehabilitation earns the comparison, and not just as a feel-good local example to end on. It's a joint venture — Tŝideldel and Tl'etinqox hold ownership themselves in the forest rehabilitation and biomass operation, with Yunesit'in territory part of the working area too. Nobody outside those Nations is writing the operating criteria for how that business runs.

Casey: That's the structural difference that actually matters. Ownership sitting inside the community doing the labor, instead of a federal loan office or an outside carbon broker writing the terms and handing them down.

Riley: And CCR has been running long enough — multiple fire seasons, not one grant cycle — to show the model holds up under real operating pressure. Compare that to Bolivia, where the carbon deals are months old and the terms are still opaque even to the people who signed them.

Casey: Time in operation is one useful test. The other test is whether the terms were ever legible to the signatories in the first place. CCR passes both, because it's the Nations' own venture from the ground up. A carbon broker showing up in Pando with a template contract passes neither.

Riley: The actual lever, then, is who's holding the pen when the eligibility rules get written in the first place — a trust board with local seats and local producers, a Nation-owned venture, versus a federal agency or an outside broker working off a template built for a different operation entirely.

Casey: That's the honest split between these stories. Ranchers First shows what happens when the pen is federal and the form doesn't fit the operation. Columbia Basin Trust and Central Chilcotin Rehabilitation show what changes when the pen moves closer to the ground it's governing. Whether that scales up to something the size of rebuilding the entire U.S. cattle herd is a much harder question, and nothing in today's material answers it.

Riley: Worth leaving open rather than forcing it. It might be a scale problem more than a pure governance problem — a regional trust works partly because the region is small enough that everyone affected can actually sit at the same table.

Casey: Which is its own limit worth naming honestly, not a solution to wave off.

Riley: If there's a concrete thing worth trying here, it's smaller than launching a new program from scratch. A Cariboo producer association could push to review loan criteria or cost-share terms before they're finalized, not after they've already been printed. Columbia Basin Trust's structure is a working template for what that review could look like locally.

Casey: And whoever ends up drafting carbon offset language for Cariboo forest tenures should be asking the Pando question up front — who in the room actually understands the contract, and who just showed up needing the cheque that week.
