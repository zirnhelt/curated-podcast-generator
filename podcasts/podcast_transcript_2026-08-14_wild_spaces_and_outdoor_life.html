Casey: Six wildfires are still out of control across the Cariboo Fire Centre as of this week. The question underneath a fire season like this one isn't just what a municipality can handle right now. It's what we owe the animals and habitats that don't get a vote in how this plays out, and won't be around to object if we get it wrong.

Riley: The numbers actually tell an interesting story if you look at the whole summer, not just this week. Late July, the Cariboo had somewhere between nine and eleven fires burning out of control at once — Barriere North Thompson Star had it at eleven on July 28th, 100 Mile Free Press had nine on both the 23rd and the 29th. By mid-August, that's down to six. That's real containment progress. Better predictive modeling, better resource allocation between fire centres, crews getting ahead of ignitions instead of chasing them.

Casey: Or it's a rotating cast — new ignitions replacing the ones that got contained, on top of a drought and snowpack baseline the province itself admits is getting structurally worse every year.

Riley: The BC Wildfire Service's own seasonal outlook does say that — persistent drought, low snowpack, elevated risk of extreme fire behavior. That's exactly why the containment number matters. If the underlying conditions are worsening and the out-of-control count is still dropping, the response is working harder to hold the line, not getting lucky.

Casey: Emergency Management Minister Kelly Greene said it herself in that same release — climate change is rewriting what counts as normal in this province. Warmer, drier conditions raising fire and drought risk together isn't a one-summer anomaly you contain your way out of. It's the baseline every year going forward, whether BCWS holds the line at six fires or sixteen.

Riley: Nobody's arguing it's not the new baseline. The question is whether better tools change what happens to the animals living inside that baseline, or whether we're just documenting the decline with more precision.

Casey: That's the sharper version of the question. For some Cariboo Mountains and Wells Gray caribou herds, the honest answer is that we're past recovery — we're managing a remnant population and calling it something gentler. Repeat burns on top of that don't reset on a normal cycle. Old-growth lichen habitat that caribou depend on in winter can take many decades — some estimates run close to a century — to regenerate after a stand-replacing fire. If fire return intervals shorten because of drought, that habitat window may never fully reopen before the next burn comes through.

Riley: Which is precisely the argument for real-time habitat mapping paired with fire behavior modeling — not to stop the fire itself, but to know in advance which specific drainages hold the last intact lichen stands, and prioritize keeping fire out of those particular pockets, even while letting other areas burn through on their own.

Casey: That assumes the resourcing to make that call exists in the moment it's needed. BCWS is fighting six fires right now with finite crews and finite aircraft. Telling incident command to divert a helicopter toward caribou habitat instead of toward a threatened structure is a real tradeoff, not a technical fix you can model your way around.

Riley: It's a tradeoff that gets made anyway, informally, by whoever's running the incident that day. The alternative to making it explicit with data isn't neutrality — it's the same decision, made blind, by whoever happens to be on shift.

Casey: Making that tradeoff explicit is better than pretending it isn't happening — but it still doesn't answer who's making the call, or whether the province funds a wildlife liaison seat inside the incident command structure in the first place. Right now that's mostly informal, if it happens at all.

Riley: It happens more than people assume — wildlife values are supposed to get flagged in provincial wildfire response planning before a season even starts. Whether that flag actually changes a suppression tactic on the ground, in the middle of an active burn, is the real gap.

Casey: Right. That's the gap.

Riley: There's a parallel worth drawing to the migrant farm workers story. That fire didn't just threaten a structure — it ended people's income and displaced them from housing tied directly to the job. Wildlife doesn't get an emergency support fund or an expedited pathway back to normal. When a burn takes out winter range, there's no equivalent of the livestock re-entry pass system Popham described for Okanagan ranchers. Once the habitat's gone, there's no return trip to feed it back to health the way you can truck feed in for cattle.

Casey: That's the honest comparison, and it cuts both ways. Farmers got a pass system because they organized, had political standing, and could pressure a regional district. Caribou, moose, salmon — none of them can lobby anyone for an exception. The obligation to think generations ahead for them falls entirely on whoever's willing to advocate on their behalf, and that advocacy competes for the same depleted provincial attention as the deficit The Tyee wrote about this week.

Riley: Winter ticks on moose are the sharpest version of that same problem. That's a climate signal showing up disguised as a parasite outbreak, and no wildlife branch engineers a colder winter into existence to break the tick cycle. What they can do is track population density and range shift with GPS collar data closely enough to know where moose are moving as habitat quality drops, and adjust hunting allocations or predator management before a population crashes instead of after it already has.

Casey: (deadpan) Which is diagnostic, again, not curative. Knowing exactly how fast a population is declining doesn't slow the decline by itself. What it does is let someone write a very precise obituary for a herd, with excellent supporting graphs.

Riley: It also lets someone intervene earlier than they otherwise would have. The Peace region caribou recovery is the actual counter-example, and it's not hypothetical — GPS telemetry combined with maternal penning took a herd from a few dozen animals up to several times that number over roughly a decade. That's not diagnosis. That's a population that exists today specifically because someone acted on the data instead of just filing it.

Casey: That program also required years of sustained funding, a dedicated penning facility, and active predator control that gets politically contested every single year it continues to run. It worked, once, in one herd, with resources most Cariboo herds have never come close to receiving.

Riley: Which is actually the closest thing to an answer here — not a monitoring tool by itself, but the willingness to fund the unglamorous, multi-year intervention the data points toward, even when it stays politically contested every season it runs. The tool tells you exactly where to put the penning facility. It doesn't build the facility for you, and it doesn't fund the decade of maintenance after that.

Casey: And that's where the province's own budget numbers cut directly against the sentiment.
