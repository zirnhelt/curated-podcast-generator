Casey: When a team pulls a planet out of Webb data that was collected years ago, have they made a discovery, or found a lead?

Riley: A discovery, if the method holds up. FLIP builds what the team calls a digital twin of Webb's NIRCam instrument, a software model of the optics. It corrects for the small ways a reference star's image fails to match the science image. And because it works from the archive, the gain is measured against the old processing on the same frames. Two to 15 times more sensitive is a number you can check today.

Casey: The proof so far is a planet everyone already knew about. 51 Eridani b was found before FLIP touched it. Recovering it at a new wavelength is a good result. It tests recovery. It doesn't test finding.

Riley: Recovery is how you calibrate a tool. A method has to see what's already there before anyone trusts it with what isn't.

Casey: Sure. The sharper version of my objection: a pipeline tuned to show one planet can also be tuned to show what the team expects to see.

Riley: Which is why astronomers usually test a pipeline by planting artificial planets in the data and checking whether it digs them back out. That's standard practice in direct imaging, and it's the test I'd want reported alongside FLIP.

Casey: Planted planets only test what you thought to plant. A faint companion at an angle nobody modelled won't show up. The planting proves the method recovers what you put in. Finding what you didn't put in is a separate claim.

Riley: It's a bounded test, and bounded still beats none. A method that fails the planting test should never reach the archive.

Casey: On that we agree. The real test is the batch of hundreds of archive images now being rerun, and those results are still pending.

Riley: And they'll arrive from data nobody has to fly again. A new mission takes years of engineering before it sees a photon. This takes computing.

Casey: Computing somebody has to keep paying for. The team says further work is needed to cut computing costs and test the method on larger data sets. Who pays for hundreds of reruns, and who pays when the next batch lands?

Riley: A server bill is much smaller than a launch. Northwestern, Caltech and MIT people built this on existing machines, without a single new mirror.

Casey: A server bill that comes due every time the archive grows. Small numbers add up when nobody budgets them.

Riley: The bigger gain may be what it frees up. Today, Webb spends time photographing other stars purely to build reference images for subtraction. If FLIP can eventually model the glare without those, that time goes to more stars and planets. Telescope time is the scarce resource on Webb. A saving there beats one sharper picture of one star.

Casey: That saving is on paper. FLIP still calibrates against a reference Webb observation first, and skipping that stage is a goal with no date attached.

Riley: The first stage is a known cost the team wants to remove. That's a design target, and targets get met or missed in public, where you can check which.

Casey: Then there's the claim about future observatories. If software can correct for optical drift, the next telescope might not need to be built quite so rigidly stable. Might is the whole claim. A stability requirement relaxed on the strength of one method is a decision somebody defends ten years later, after the method has changed.

Riley: Every method starts at might. What matters is whether the next observatory gets specified looser because someone showed this working on a real archive. That lever sits with the people writing the specifications.

Casey: And those people get asked to fund the consequences later.

Casey: Neptune is the harder case. That oxygen-rich origin story from up top rests on a model, and the outside check the researchers point to is NASA's Uranus Orbiter and Probe.

Riley: Inference is how planetary science works when you can't reach the thing. The team measured a chemical difference between two planets of similar size and mass and built a formation story from it. The oxygen gap is the part a probe can test directly, which makes the claim testable at all.

Casey: When does that probe launch?

Riley: The earliest window is June 2031, with a backup in April 2032. NASA said in 2023 that the mid-to-late 2030s is more likely, because of plutonium shortfalls for its power source.

Casey: So a launch, a cruise out past Saturn, the measurements, then the analysis. And Uranus only answers half the question. Waiting on it is no plan for being right any sooner.

Riley: Then label it an inference, publish the model with the claim, and let the probe test it. The decadal survey ranked that mission the top large planetary priority for a reason. Holding the result for fifteen years helps no one.

Casey: Labels are the first thing dropped. By the time a result is a headline, the model has fallen off and the claim sounds like a measurement.

Riley: Then the label matters more when the wait is long. A label costs a sentence. A probe costs a launch vehicle.

Casey: Who checks the label after the headline moves on?

Riley: The probe, eventually. Until then, the label tells a reader which parts are models.

Casey: And the Neptune inference goes years with no check from outside the team. I'd want that written into the press release in plain words.

Riley: The CERN result is the same job at the opposite scale. Pulling a faint signal out of a noisy collision is pulling a planet out of a noisy telescope image. The decay is rare enough that seeing it at all counts as the result.

Casey: The signal is the result. "First step" and "glimpse" are forecasts. WIRED uses them to open up a scale nobody has explored yet, and a reader can mistake a forecast for a finding.

Riley: The physics is well defined, though. A muon is about 200 times heavier than an electron, and the neutral B meson lasts only a sliver of a second before it decays. After four decades of looking, a rate that low is hard to fake by accident.

Casey: Rare enough that one signal in a huge dataset needs a strict statistical bar before anyone calls it real. That bar is what I'd want a reader told about.

Riley: Particle physicists set that bar before they announce, and it's stricter than most fields use. A decay that rare, cleared against that bar, is what a first step looks like.

Casey: Cut the adjectives, keep the signal. The zeptoverse is still a plan.

Riley: A plan with a working detector behind it. Most plans don't get that footing.

Casey: The detector I'll grant. The plan is still a plan.

Casey: That leaves the sky you can look at yourself. The Cariboo has dark skies, and on a clear night out past Williams Lake, the light you see arrives unedited. Webb's view has been cleaned up by a model before it reaches a screen.

A dark sky gets you into the subject. It doesn't compete with Webb as evidence.

Riley: Then the check has to be a second team running the same method on the same archive. That fix is structural, and it needs no heroics. Many Webb programs release their data publicly after an exclusive period, with terms that vary by program, so the raw frames are already there.

Casey: Sitting in an archive and ready to use are two different states. Someone needs the software, the calibration files, and time on a machine. Does a small team outside a big institution ever get that time?

Riley: The team, the probe, or a second group. Each is a check, and none is free. Only a second group can tell the first one no.

Casey: And if each assumes somebody else is checking, nobody is. I'd want that failure named in the next budget line.

Riley: Name it, then fix it. The fix costs less than the failure.

Casey: In my experience the archive gets the bill after the announcement, every time.

Riley: So move the cost up front. Require the planted-planet test in the paper, and require the reprocessed archive to be open before the headline runs.

Casey: Schweitzer's objection to the dinosaur paper fits here. Whole-bone chemistry and whole-archive reprocessing share a weakness: the average can hide the one thing you were looking for.

Riley: Which is why the reprocessing should be checked by someone who didn't choose the settings. Someone with a different reason to get the answer right.

Casey: Someone who loses nothing if the result is smaller than advertised. Those people are rarer than telescope time.
