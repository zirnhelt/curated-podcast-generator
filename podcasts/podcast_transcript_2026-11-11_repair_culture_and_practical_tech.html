Casey: When a phone breaks, where does repair stop making sense for a household? And who sets that line, the owner doing the sums or the company pricing the parts?

Riley: The owner, if the sums are honest. Android Authority worked through that exact calculation and concluded that sometimes spending hundreds on a repair is the smarter financial decision. Past a threshold, buying a new handset is the better spend. A household can find that line with three numbers: the repair quote, the phone's resale value, and the price of a replacement.

Casey: Sometimes. The article's own example is the iPhone Duo, where a display replacement is said to cost as much as a new iPhone 18 Pro. So the threshold is set by a price somebody else chose.

Riley: Which the owner can't negotiate, so the sum has to be run against it. What matters is whether that number is fixed or whether service design moves it. NewsBytes and RepairNestPOS both report Apple charging $999 out of warranty for the inner display, or $99 with AppleCare+. That's a tenfold spread on the same part.

Casey: Reported figures. They aren't on a price list Apple has published. And they don't stand alone. Ricky Panesar, the founder of the repair firm iCorrect, told Forbes he expects about £850, roughly $1,155, for the inner screen. He put the same repair on a Galaxy Z Fold 8 at £594. Notebookcheck and TrendForce carried the same estimate, and TweakTown called it possibly the most expensive iPhone screen repair ever.

Riley: So the gap between the two foldables is about £256 on the inner screen alone. Some of that is physics. A folding inner panel is harder to make and harder to swap than a flat screen. But the AppleCare+ figure shows the same part can reach a household at a price it can handle when the service is designed for it.

Casey: Nothing says repairable like a parts price that needs an insurance plan to be reasonable.

Riley: Ha. Still, coverage is a choice made at checkout, so a household that reads the terms can change its own outcome.

Casey: At checkout, and only if the plan is on offer when they're buying. Who ends up at the $999 line? Mostly people who skipped the plan, or bought the phone before they knew the plan mattered. They're often the ones least able to pay it.

Riley: And how much is $999 against the phone itself?

Casey: The reported starting price for the Duo is $1,999, so the inner screen is half the phone. For comparison, the standard iPhone 18 Pro's out-of-warranty screen repair is $329, and $419 if the back glass goes too.

Riley: Then the flat-screen phone and the foldable sit in different worlds, even from the same company.

Casey: And the estimates scatter. An iFixPros estimate from a Dallas-Fort Worth repair shop put the out-of-warranty foldable repair at $500 to $900, against $129 to $329 for a standard iPhone screen. That was before launch. PhoneArena reports up to $1,499 for some foldable repairs, with the inner display at $479 in one comparison, though those inner-display figures appear to belong to a different foldable. Every one of these numbers is getting quoted as a price.

Riley: The $1,499 does match one line in the reported Duo schedule: other accidental damage, up to $1,499 without coverage, $99 with it. The outer display is reported at $379, or $29 covered. The back glass is $199, or $29. Across every line, coverage cuts the bill to a fraction.

Casey: Which makes the plan a toll. You pay it up front or you pay the full price later.

Riley: Or you pay nothing and the phone never breaks. People do get lucky. A household can still do the sums with the best number available.

Casey: The best number available is a guess that gets repeated until it sounds like a price list.

Riley: Then the policy lever matters more than the checkout counter. The EU's ecodesign rules for smartphones and tablets, Regulation 2023/1670, took effect June 20th, 2025. They cover energy efficiency, durability, repairability and spare parts. The Right to Repair Directive requires member states to adopt at least one measure that encourages repair, such as repair vouchers. And the Batteries Regulation requires user-removable or easily replaceable batteries in mobile devices, with the rules applying from 2027. Vouchers and parts rules are law now. Advocates aren't just asking for them anymore.

Casey: Those ecodesign rules apply to devices first placed on the EU market on or after June 20th, 2025. A household holding a three-year-old handset gets nothing from them.

Riley: Right, and the installed base is the slow part. Still, a rule that applies to new devices changes what the next generation is built to survive.

Casey: Only if it changes what gets built. A removable battery doesn't touch a folding panel. The screen drives the cost.

Riley: Batteries are the repair people do most often, though. A cheaper battery swap keeps the phone out of the drawer for another year, even if the screen is the expensive part.

Casey: Fine. But TechRadar, reporting a year after the ecodesign rules took effect, still couldn't say whether phones were actually getting easier to repair. It also reported that Apple's new CEO, John Ternus, isn't fully convinced about repairability. The law is new, and the evidence is thin.

Riley: The law is new. The engineering trend toward sealed, integrated designs is older, and the rule is trying to reverse it. Give it a few product cycles before calling it a failure.

Casey: Meanwhile the household is still doing the math alone.

Riley: Which is why the math has to be the right kind. Android Authority compares repair costs with and without insurance to estimate what a handset costs to own as parts prices climb. A single repair quote tells you one number. A cost-of-ownership figure tells you whether the phone is worth keeping past its second repair.

Casey: The piece also carries an affiliate disclosure. Android Authority may earn a commission from some links. Still useful. Just know whose numbers you're trusting.

Riley: Agreed. The article also says smartphone innovation has plateaued, and calls that a blessing in disguise for consumers. If the new phone isn't much better, the old one gives up little by staying in your pocket.

Casey: The same article describes spare parts prices that keep climbing. So the longer you hold a phone, the more each repair costs against a replacement. The plateau argument and the parts argument pull in opposite directions, and the household is stuck between them.

Riley: And the insurance comparison is trying to put both pressures in one place: the total cost of owning the handset. With the Duo, that's $99 a claim with coverage, or $999 for one inner screen without it.

Casey: One number that assumes a shop is down the street.

Riley: For a household in Quesnel, Williams Lake or 100 Mile House, the sum needs lines the article doesn't price. Mail the phone out and you pay shipping both ways, and you wait while it sits in a courier's bin. Drive it to a counter and you pay fuel and lose a day. The repair math is the same. The inputs are different.

Casey: And nobody quotes the time a phone spends in a mailbag. A cracked screen on a working phone is a week of a backup phone, a borrowed charger, and a family arguing over the one that works.

Riley: So add your own distance to the sum. Fuel, a day off work, the courier, the days without the phone. Put a number on each before you compare it with the quote.

Casey: The wait gets a number too. Nobody budgets for it.

Riley: Right.

Casey: And the gap that won't close is this. The Duo's inner screen is quoted anywhere from $999 to about $1,155 out of warranty, depending on which source you read. The AppleCare+ figure is $99. A household in 100 Mile House could run the math perfectly and still have no repair counter within reach that will touch a foldable. Then the sum doesn't matter.

Riley: Then the gap is structural, and parts of it are solvable locally. That renovated library branch we mentioned up top is the kind of space where a monthly repair night could happen, a place to bring a phone, a kettle or a lamp. Pair that with a published parts-price list and you have a working experiment for a winter.

Casey: Not a bad start. But a repair night is volunteers and a bench. Who fixes the foldable? You need a technician with the parts and the training, and a repair night supplies neither.

Riley: Agreed. The repair night is where a household learns what a foldable actually costs to fix before it decides. That number is the start.

Casey: So the first thing a household needs is a price it can check, and the manufacturer isn't offering one.

Riley: Then the first fix is a published parts-price list. The second is an independent tester checking it, someone other than the company selling the phone. If iCorrect can estimate the inner screen at £850, a third party can publish the same figure with a date and a source attached.

Casey: Then the owner's sums are only as good as the list, and nobody's been assigned to check it. We raised that same worry on Sunday, about a different system. It fits here too.

Riley: And the cost lands in the same place. A household gets a repair quote that looks reasonable and a replacement that looks cheaper, and never sees the whole picture. The sum comes out wrong, and nobody gets blamed for it.
