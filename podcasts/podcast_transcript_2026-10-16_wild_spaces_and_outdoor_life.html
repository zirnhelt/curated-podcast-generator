Riley: When the smoke comes down the canyon, who gets to drive out, and who has to wait it out? Start with the number. Between 2000 and 2019, the count of people exposed to wildfire and extreme heat on the same days rose by 287 million. Inside Climate News calls it the largest global assessment of that overlap to date, published in Science Advances. The annual average went from about 761 million people in the first half of the period to over a billion in the second.

Casey: That's modeled exposure. Same-day overlap isn't a health outcome. What share of the 287 million comes from hazard change, and what share from more people living in hot, fire-prone places? That split decides whether this is a climate story or a settlement story.

Riley: Both are in it. A related Science Advances paper covered the Western US from 2006 to 2020 and mapped co-exposure to extreme heat, burn zones, and smoke at census-tract level. That's the resolution an evacuation planner needs. You can't warn a tract you haven't mapped.

Casey: Mapping tells you a tract is exposed. It doesn't tell you whether anyone in it has a running vehicle, a bed to go to, or a neighbour who'll pick up the phone.

Riley: Then the map is step one of three. Alerts are step two. Transport and shelter are step three, and that's where the planning usually runs out. Nobody argues against step three. The argument is about who pays for it.

Casey: Take 100 Mile House, around 1,900 people. The highway closes and the power goes out on the same afternoon. Who runs the shelter, and who drives the people with no car?

Riley: Planning can answer that. Name the shelter before the fire, pre-register the people who need rides, and rehearse it. Most small communities do this rarely, if at all.

Casey: Rehearsal costs money every year. A binder doesn't get a driver.

Riley: Then budget for the driver. That's a line item, not a mystery.

Casey: Which channels? Phone platforms set their own rules for emergency messages. A warning that depends on a company's terms is only as reliable as those terms.

Riley: Prop 45 is the other half of this. It's on the November ballot, and it would amend CEQA, the state's landmark environmental review law. It sets deadlines for environmental review and for resolving lawsuits against approvals. It covers most housing, transportation, water, and health projects. The California Chamber of Commerce backs it. Opponents include environmental groups, some labor unions, and the California Democratic Party. They reported raising about $15.6 million, with major money from philanthropist Wendy Schmidt and several labor and environmental groups.

Casey: The New York Times piece quotes Christopher Elmendorf, a land-use law professor at UC Davis, who says the Chamber is effectively asking for the growth California had in the 1960s. That's edge-city growth. And the edge is where the fire is.

Riley: The strongest case for it is simple. CEQA lawsuits have made close-in infill slow and expensive. When building gets stuck inside the city, it moves to cheaper land, and the cheap land is the wildland-urban interface. Faster approvals reduce the pressure to push homes into the places that burn.

Casey: Faster approvals don't stop builders from choosing fire-prone land. They make it quicker to get permits there. The clock speeds up everywhere, including the places where the risk is already on the record.

Riley: The answer is in the structure, not the clock. A review that ends with a required fire-hardening plan differs from one that ends in a lawsuit. The question is whether review in fire-prone ground does real work or just adds years.

Casey: Who writes that plan? And who checks it in year five, after the permit is issued and the lawyers have moved on?

Riley: The local government. The same people who'd check it under the old system.
