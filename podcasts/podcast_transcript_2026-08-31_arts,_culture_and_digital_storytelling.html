Riley: Australia changed its music charts this past Friday. ARIA's updated Charts Code of Practice now excludes any track that isn't, in their language, substantially human made. A human has to write the song. A human has to perform lead vocals or the primary instruments. AI can sit in a supporting production role — mixing, mastering, some backing texture — but it can't be the performer. And the AI tools involved have to be authorized and lawful, meaning trained on licensed material, not scraped off an artist's back catalogue without permission.

Casey: And the reason ARIA moved at all is one specific DJ. Josh Fawaz put out a fully AI-generated cover of Madonna's "Like a Prayer," and it topped Australian radio play in the middle of the summer, before anyone had a rule against it.

Riley: Millions of streams before the ban existed in any form. But it's not just Australia acting alone here. The IFPI — the international body representing record labels globally — issued similar principles back in July. The Recording Academy already has a Grammy rule barring fully AI-generated songs from award eligibility, while still allowing AI-assisted work as long as there's meaningful human authorship behind it. Three institutions, three different countries and mandates, landing on nearly the same definition within weeks of each other.

Casey: Landing on the same definition on paper. Billboard is a different story. Major labels — Universal, Sony, Warner — proposed the same substantially human-made standard for U.S. charts. But reporting suggests fully AI-generated tracks are still climbing genre-specific Billboard charts right now, while that proposal sits unresolved. The same song could be banned from an Australian chart and climbing a U.S. one at the same moment.

Riley: That's a rollout lag, not a philosophical disagreement. Big bureaucratic chart systems with decades of legacy processes move slower than a single national body updating one code of practice. Give it a cycle or two.

Casey: Or it never converges, because the U.S. market is big enough that nobody with a hit needs Australia's charts to matter. A patchwork where enforcement depends on which country's chart you're aiming at isn't a transition phase. It's the permanent shape of the thing.

Riley: Which gets at something worth putting plainly. Is the goal here to be self-sufficient — making something entirely your own hands and voice can account for — or is the goal to be well-connected, plugged into whatever chart and platform system actually gets you heard? Right now those two goals pull in opposite directions.

Casey: They were never that close together to start with. A songwriter in Horsefly, writing and recording something entirely with their own hands, still needs Spotify, or terrestrial radio, or a chart listing, to reach anyone outside the valley. Being self-sufficient in production buys you nothing in reach.

Riley: That's precisely why the ARIA rule should matter more to a rural artist than to a major-label act. If charts start requiring provable human authorship, the artist who actually writes and performs their own material has a credential the AI-generated cover doesn't. It rewards the kind of work rural musicians are already doing out of necessity, since most of them don't have the budget for a synthetic vocal pipeline anyway.
