Riley: Venus had oceans across ninety percent of its surface, maybe for two billion years, and now it's four hundred and sixty degrees under a crushing carbon dioxide atmosphere. The interesting question isn't really Venus — it's how confident we should be that our own models of Earth's water and ice systems have already found the signals that matter.

Casey: Because we've been burned by that kind of overconfidence before, and not just on Venus.

Riley: That's the ALMA story too — same principle, completely different instrument. Decades of SETI searches concentrated on one narrow radio band, the so-called waterhole, mostly because it was convenient and well-understood, not because anyone proved that's where a signal would actually show up. Researchers ran the first SETI survey ever conducted on ALMA's archived data, in a much higher and cleaner frequency range, and found that a handful of existing observations already covered millions of stars nobody had properly searched before.

Casey: Still no signal, though. Still nothing.

Riley: Still nothing. But the point isn't the null result — it's that an entire fifty-year methodology assumed it had covered the meaningful search space, and it hadn't. That's a bigger miss than anyone in the field likes to admit out loud.

Casey: Except "we were listening in the wrong place" is exactly what SETI researchers said about Dyson spheres, about laser pulse detection, about half a dozen other technosignature categories over the decades. Every null result gets repackaged as "we just haven't looked in the right spot yet." At some point that stops being a methodology correction and starts looking like a coping mechanism for a field that keeps coming up empty.

Riley: That skepticism holds for astronomy specifically. Apply the same failure mode to Earth systems, though, and it stops being comforting and starts being useful. The Cariboo Mountains icefields feeding the Quesnel and Fraser watersheds have decades of periodic aerial survey photographs, but continuous mass-balance monitoring — the kind of measurement that tells you precisely how fast a glacier is losing ice year over year, not just that the general trend is downward — appears thin on the ground, by most accounts. We know the direction. We don't have the resolution to say which specific years are catching the system off guard.

Casey: And that's the comparison worth making — not "aliens versus glaciers" as some cute parallel, but "what counts as adequate monitoring versus what we've settled for because it was cheap and easy to fund." The ALMA team got lucky that ALMA's archive already existed for a completely different astronomical purpose and could be repurposed for SETI at basically no additional cost. Nobody built a glacier-monitoring network for the Cariboo Mountains as a side effect of something else already being funded.

Riley: That's the stronger version of your argument — instruments built for one purpose ending up revealing something nobody was originally looking for. Which, oddly enough, is exactly what happened with the battery research out of Surrey. Engineers have spent years deliberately drying sodium vanadium oxide before using it as a battery electrode, because the working assumption was that trapped water degraded performance over time. Turns out leaving the water in nearly doubles the material's storage capacity, and the same crystal structure works as a desalination electrode when reconfigured.

Casey: A decades-old manufacturing assumption, apparently never seriously re-tested, quietly wrong the entire time.

Riley: And it matters here specifically because of water, not just batteries. A material that stores grid power and pulls salt out of seawater using the same underlying structure is relevant to any drought-stressed system — including a region already watching Hungary shut down a nuclear reactor because its river can't cool it anymore.

Casey: Lab-scale cathode chemistry, one peer-reviewed paper, full stop — that caveat needs to stay attached whenever this comes up again. Silicon-carbon batteries got the exact same "doubles the power" framing in smartphones for a couple of years before it showed up in actual consumer devices as an incremental gain, not the revolution the headlines promised. Sodium-ion chemistry has overpromised against lithium-ion commercially, repeatedly, for a decade now. This is genuinely interesting science. It is nowhere near deployable.

Riley: No argument there — it's years from a pilot plant, let alone from a well system out past Horsefly.
