Casey: The FCC gave itself a new power on October 13th. Under it, the agency can bar new equipment authorizations for products containing logic-bearing hardware made by companies on its Covered List. On October 22nd it used that power for the first time, blocking two devices it says contain foreign surveillance chips. Both use a HiSilicon chip, which the FCC describes as a Huawei subsidiary. So if a regulator won't say which gadgets it blocked, and a payment firm can change its name after sanctions, what can a household in the Cariboo actually check before it trusts a product?

Riley: The chip is named, though. HiVision Hi3516CV610, a smart vision system-on-chip. A repair shop can read a part number off a board and compare it to a list. A rule that works by component moves faster than investigating each finished product one at a time.

Casey: Named in one place. The Verge reports the agency hasn't said which gadgets were blocked. Other coverage describes a dashboard camera and a wireless video transmitter. Two devices, one chip. You can check the chip on a board, but you can't check it on a shelf.
