Riley: Nominations closed earlier this month. Five weeks left before the vote on October 17th. And sitting underneath all the candidate counts is a question this ballot actually forces — when a council term ends, who decides which parts of it were worth keeping, and which parts get erased the moment someone new takes the chair?

Casey: Because voters don't get to keep the parts they liked and toss the rest. They get a whole person, record and all.

Riley: Which makes Williams Lake the clearest test case this year. Two names for mayor, fifteen for council. One of the mayoral candidates is Walt Cobb.

Casey: Cobb's not new to this. Mayor from 1990 to 1996. Mayor again from 2014 to 2022. Now he's back looking for a fourth term. Two former mayors on one ballot — a sequel nobody pitched, and the whole town's going to watch it anyway.

Riley: And the man running against him is the man who beat him last time. Surinderpal Rathor took the seat from Cobb in October 2022 with roughly thirty-four percent of the vote in a four-candidate field — somewhere in the range of eleven hundred votes. Jason Ryll, a councillor at the time, came second with about twenty-eight percent. Cobb finished behind that.

Casey: Thirty-four percent in a four-way split is a plurality, not a mandate. Two-thirds of the people who voted in 2022 picked someone else, which is the opening Cobb is running at — a straight two-way race changes that arithmetic completely.

Riley: It also raises the stakes on Rathor's record, because a two-way race means the record is the ballot question. When he announced in June, he pointed at cutting the city's debt by four million dollars and reducing the 2025 mill rate.

Casey: Checkable claims, and they should get tested at an all-candidates forum rather than repeated from a press release. Debt reduction alone doesn't tell you whether services held while it happened, or whether it came out of deferred maintenance that lands as a bill in 2028.

Riley: Probe it, absolutely — but the deferred-maintenance theory runs into something from this past spring. Rathor was publicly pressing council and Victoria on stalled local infrastructure projects, which is the opposite behaviour from a mayor quietly letting capital work rot to make a debt number look good.

Casey: Pressing Victoria is free. Funding the work yourself isn't. A mayor can do both — trim locally and lobby upward for someone else's money — and voters can't tell those apart from a mill rate alone.

Riley: Which is why the rest of the record matters too, including the less flattering parts. A formal complaint of conflict of interest was filed against Rathor while he was still a councillor. Council held an in-camera meeting, passed a resolution letting him respond publicly, and he denied any conflict.

Casey: And there's an older item that's murkier. Rathor was arrested and charged with obstruction of justice around 2012, tied to volunteer work he did as an RCMP translator on a Christmas Eve assault case. He told reporters at the time he was still in shock.

Riley: We couldn't find a resolution to that charge anywhere in the sources we checked — no trial record, no stay, no acquittal. That stays an open item. It's an allegation from more than a decade ago, not a finding, and reporting it as anything more would be wrong.
