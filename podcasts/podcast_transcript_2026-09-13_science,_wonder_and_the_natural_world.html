Riley: Ottawa is moving to let major infrastructure projects bypass the Species at Risk Act, if they're designated projects of "national interest" under the new Building Canada Act. Environmental groups are running ad campaigns about Southern Resident killer whales. This isn't just activist messaging — the U.S. Marine Mammal Commission, an independent federal agency, has raised its own formal concerns.

Casey: Cross-border concern from a government body. That's a different category than an op-ed writer with an axe to grind.

Riley: The case in question is the Roberts Bank Terminal 2 expansion in the Fraser estuary, plus a proposed Alberta pipeline feeding into the same corridor. Federal environmental assessments already found the terminal infrastructure interferes with Chinook salmon migration.

Casey: Which is the whales' primary food source.

Riley: So the real question isn't abstract — whether removing the review requirement is a genuine tradeoff between progress and nature, or a way of overriding a finding that's already settled science.

Casey: What stands out about the mechanism is that the whole point of exempting a project from species-at-risk review is to avoid the friction that review would create. Nobody designs mitigation around a finding once the project's declared exempt from having to answer to it.

Riley: That's not automatically the wrong call, though. Mitigation technology exists — staged construction windows timed around salmon runs, acoustic dampening for vessel traffic, monitoring buoys that trigger slowdowns the moment whales are detected. Engineering can reduce harm without stopping the project outright.

Casey: If anyone's required to use it. That's the whole argument for the exemption existing — so nobody has to.

Riley: Reasonable to be wary of that. But mitigation only gets designed and funded when someone's forced to sit with a finding long enough to solve it. Remove the requirement, and you don't just cut paperwork — you remove the incentive to engineer around the problem at all.

Casey: That's the shape of the real question underneath this whole decision. Every friction point in a process like environmental review is also the mechanism that produced the practice — the studies, the staged construction windows, the monitoring stations. Make it easier to build, and you don't just save time. You lose the muscle that only ever got built because the friction demanded it.

Riley: Southern Resident killer whales are down to a few dozen individuals total. That's not a group that recovers from a policy experiment gone wrong. There's no margin left for trial and error.

Casey: And Chinook salmon runs on the Fraser system pass close enough to Cariboo territory that this isn't a coastal abstraction for us. Those fish move through waters this region depends on long before they reach the whales waiting at the river mouth.

Riley: That's the piece that gets lost when a Vancouver op-ed frames this as purely a coastal issue. The Fraser is one watershed, start to finish. What happens at Roberts Bank touches the same system that Cariboo Nations have monitored and depended on for generations further upstream.

Casey: If federal assessments already found interference with Chinook migration, and that gets waved through anyway, the exemption isn't bypassing an unknown risk. It's bypassing a documented finding.

Riley: This isn't "we don't have enough science yet, let's proceed cautiously." The assessment exists. The finding exists. The exemption is built so that finding doesn't have to be answered.

Casey: Which makes this a different animal than a normal cost-benefit tradeoff. A tradeoff implies weighing two real things against each other honestly. This is one side declaring itself outside the weighing altogether.
