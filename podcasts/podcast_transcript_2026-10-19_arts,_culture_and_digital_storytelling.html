Casey: The State Department started sending jazz musicians abroad in 1955 and 1956. Armstrong, Dizzy Gillespie, Duke Ellington, and Dave Brubeck all went. When a band walks onto a stage in another country, whose credibility is on the line? The musicians spent decades building it, and the government bought a share. So is a reputation something a person builds on the bandstand, or something a sponsor can inherit and spend?

Riley: Built on the bandstand, at least at first. An audience that has only heard propaganda about America hears a Black trumpet player and a working band, and the picture changes in the room. Time and PBS document the routes through Eastern Europe, the Middle East, South Asia, and newly decolonized Africa and Asia. No ministry can write that picture. The audience builds it.

Casey: And the room had a price tag on it.

Riley: It had a cost, and the musicians knew it. Casey: Armstrong toured Italy in 1931, under Mussolini. The Council on Foreign Relations documents his 1960 goodwill concert in Leopoldville, in the Congo. Jazz was travelling long before the program existed.
