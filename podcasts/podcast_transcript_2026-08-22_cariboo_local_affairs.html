Casey: There's a real question sitting underneath today's stories. When Ottawa and Washington negotiate a trade deal, does anyone in the Cariboo actually get a seat, or does the region just find out afterward what its own economy is going to look like?

Riley: The softwood lumber story is the clean example. Commerce Department numbers released this week show the combined duty rate on Canadian softwood dropping from around thirty-six percent to roughly twenty-five. The preliminary antidumping component alone fell from twenty point six percent down to ten point seven.

Casey: That's real money on paper. It's also still a quarter of the sale price of the product that built half the towns in this region, gone before a mill ever sees a dollar.

Riley: The direction is what matters here. Carney and Trump have been in active talks, and this is the first measurable movement in the actual number, not another promise that a deal is close.

Casey: People have been told a deal is close for two years running. Meanwhile Drax closed its pellet plant, 100 Mile House lost its mill outright, Quesnel River Pulp curtailed production. A hundred and sixty-five jobs here, ninety there. Those losses didn't wait around for the rate to drop.

Riley: And even this relief has a catch built into it. Some finished Canadian wood products, cabinets and millwork specifically, are facing escalating tariffs climbing toward thirty to fifty percent by January. So raw lumber eases while the value-added side of the same industry gets squeezed harder.

Casey: Which is the actual governance problem, isn't it. No council in Quesnel or Williams Lake voted on a single one of these numbers. The towns living with the layoffs have zero formal say in any of it.

Riley: They never did. Trade policy sits with the federal government, full stop. The real question is whether local government builds any leverage of its own instead of just absorbing whatever rate lands on it.

Casey: What would leverage even look like for a town of nine thousand people staring down a national trade dispute?

Riley: Value-added processing is one path. A mill shipping raw dimensional lumber is completely exposed to whatever the U.S. duty happens to be that quarter. A mill that also produces engineered wood products or furniture components has a second, less exposed market.

Casey: That takes capital most of these towns don't have sitting around, and we've landed on the funding conversation three times this month already. Let's not go there again.

Riley: Fair enough, skip the money question. The lever that doesn't need new capital is procurement policy. Regional districts and the province could commit to sourcing wood products locally for public construction — schools, arenas, housing builds. That's a purchasing decision within existing municipal and provincial authority, right now, with no new funding required.
