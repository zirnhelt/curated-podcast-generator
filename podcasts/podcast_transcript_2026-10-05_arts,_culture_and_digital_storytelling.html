Casey: When a funder gives a festival or a reading program money and then asks how many people came, what was that number supposed to be standing in for?

Riley: Value. Reach. Somebody has to compare forty applications on a Tuesday, and a ticket count is the one number everybody reads the same way. A count is a floor. It tells you whether anyone showed up at all.

Casey: A floor, sure. Then it becomes the ceiling. Look at how the Toy Run was reported. Thirty-one motorcycles. Nobody involved thinks the point of the day was thirty-one bikes. But the number is what made the headline.

Riley: Because it's concrete. Thirty-one is a figure the audience can picture, and it can be compared to last year's.

Casey: They can picture a ride. They can't picture what a toy did in a living room. The number survives and the thing it was for drops out.

Riley: Drop the count altogether and what do you have? Narrative reports. Every grant application becomes a gorgeous story, and the funder can't tell which one is true. Counting at least has the discipline of someone being able to check.

Casey: Can they check, though? Ticket counts in a town this size are self-reported by the people who need the next grant.

Riley: Podcasts and web stories have it worse and better. Worse, because the numbers are easy to inflate. Better, because you can see repeat listeners and returning readers, which is closer to attention than a one-off ticket. Count the people who come back, not the people who showed up once.

Casey: And then people optimize for coming back. Make it a habit, make it a streak. A metric aimed at attention ends up manufacturing attention. The Lancet series is a pile of evidence on exactly that: data on nine million people under 25 and what happens when products are tuned to hold young eyes.

Riley: Ha.

Casey: The authors tie it to harms from nearsightedness to self-harm. Nobody who built the engagement dashboard was trying to cause either. The dashboard counted time on screen, and time on screen became the goal.

Riley: That's the extreme case. A library story hour doesn't have an algorithm. Repeat attendance at a reading program is somebody choosing to come back on a cold Tuesday. The proxy there is about as honest as proxies get.

Casey: Until the funder wants it to grow every year. Growth is where a count stops measuring and starts demanding.

Riley: Growth targets do distort. A flat line with steady attendance can be a success, and funders could say so in writing.

Casey: Look at the Mother Jones piece. David Corn describes being dragged into a Russian disinformation operation built to look like real journalism. What makes it spread is the same thing that makes real journalism spread.

Riley: Which is why engagement is a bad measure of a newsroom. But nobody running a Cariboo paper is competing with a troll farm for clicks. The relevant question is whether the Tribune can show a funder or advertiser that people read it.

Casey: The Tribune has a thin newsroom. It's the outfit sitting through the council meeting. If its value gets measured in page views, the story about the sewer budget loses to the story about the motorcycles every time. The sewer budget is the one that affects your bill.

Riley: Page views are one input. Subscriptions, letters to the editor, a council member who changed a vote because of something in print. That last one is rare to see counted.

Casey: It's never counted. The thing a local paper is for, someone in power being watched, produces no number at all. A story that never gets written because an official knew someone was watching doesn't show up in analytics.

Riley: So what's the alternative? A funder can't hand out money on "trust us, the council behaves."

Casey: I don't have an alternative that scales. I have a complaint about the one that does.

Riley: Complaints don't write grant criteria.

Casey: Neither do headcounts. They just look like they do.
