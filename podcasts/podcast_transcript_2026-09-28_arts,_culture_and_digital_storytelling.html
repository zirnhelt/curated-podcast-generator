DEEP DIVE: CARIBOO CONNECTIONS - Arts, Culture & Digital Storytelling

Casey: Start with the question underneath all of it. Staff at the V&A are striking over conditions, and museums across Europe are being robbed by people who are in and out fast. So is a tiny volunteer-run gallery up here the safer bet, because it's small and personal and nobody's running it like a warehouse? Or is "small and volunteer-run" just the polite phrase for underfunded and one bad year from folding?

Riley: The scale argument cuts both ways, and that's the part nobody wants to sit in. The V&A has centuries of institutional weight, a payroll department, a whole management layer — and its own staff still walked out calling the conditions "Amazon-style." Being big doesn't buy you good management. It buys you more people around to be disappointed by it.

Casey: Big also buys you a security budget. The Hyperallergic piece on the European theft wave makes the point without much subtlety: these institutions are struggling to protect irreplaceable collections after years of underfunding, and the thefts land hardest on the places that aren't flagships.

Riley: The Louvre got robbed too, and that's not an under-resourced institution.

Casey: Once, and it made global news for a week. That piece is explicitly about the thefts that are bigger than the Louvre story — the ones at smaller and mid-tier museums that never crack international headlines, because nobody's photographing the aftermath for the wire services.

Riley: Small can move faster on the exact things that prevent theft, though — better locks, a tighter rotation schedule, knowing every volunteer by name and face. The V&A can't retrain its entire security staff overnight. A three-person board can change the locks by Thursday if they decide it matters.

Casey: Can they afford the locks?

Riley: Yes, and that's the underrated part. Decent hardware-store locks and a couple of cameras are a hardware-store expense, not a security-consultancy retainer. The financial barrier at the small end is much lower than people assume — the barrier is usually the decision, not the invoice.

Casey: The barrier is that a volunteer board running things off the side of their own desks won't conduct a security audit unless something's already gone wrong. Nobody schedules the fire drill for the fire that hasn't happened.

Riley: Reactive versus proactive — and small organizations skew reactive because no one's job description says "think about this before it's a problem." Which is why the fix I'd push isn't a consultant. A regional museum running on volunteers needs a complete photographic inventory of its own collection, held somewhere other than the building itself.

Casey: Why does that beat the lock on the door?

Riley: Because recovery odds after a theft depend almost entirely on documentation good enough to match against something like Interpol's stolen art database, or against an insurance claim. Plenty of small museums don't have that in usable form — not because it's expensive, but because nobody's ever been assigned to sit down and do it.

Casey: Assigning a task to a volunteer board doesn't make the hours materialize. And the Bayeux Tapestry protest is a different vulnerability entirely — that wasn't a broken lock, it was a governance failure over who gets to attach their name and money to a cultural object. Tulchinsky's financing tied to a settlement in the Occupied West Bank wasn't a security question. It was whether anyone checks before the sponsorship deal is signed.

Riley: Same question underneath both, though — who has the authority to decide what's acceptable, and does anyone check before the money's spent and the exhibit's hung. And a small organization is where that authority is clearest. There's no committee to hide behind. Three people know exactly who said yes.

Casey: Clarity isn't leverage. The V&A can lose a sponsor over bad press without blinking. A gallery running on three volunteers can't, so "we know who said yes" mostly tells you who had no choice. Small is more accountable only if the small organization can afford to refuse money it needs. Otherwise it isn't principled, it's cornered.

Riley: Which is where Surrey is instructive. The province and the city just settled that Surrey will host the long-awaited B.C. South Asian heritage museum, and the two of them now work together to find a site. That's what political will plus a real budget line produces — staffing and security and provenance planning built in at the start instead of bolted on after a scare.

Casey: They have a winning bid and no building. "Now work together to find a site" is the polite phase of a project, the one before land costs and consultation and a change of government. Big projects fail differently than small ones — they fail slowly, in procurement, with everyone still employed. I wouldn't hold that up as the finished counterexample yet.

Riley: Fine, it's unbuilt. It's also a museum that exists on a provincial ledger, which is more than most community collections up here can say. And "whoever showed up and stayed" describes a huge share of the infrastructure in this region, arts or otherwise, and most of it has worked for decades without a budget line.

Casey: Until the one person who understood the alarm system retires and nobody was ever taught it. That's how small institutions actually fail — not with a fire or a break-in on the news, just quietly, one missing person at a time, until the gap shows up after something's already gone.

Riley: So the dividing line isn't size. It's whether the institutional knowledge lives in one person's head or is written down somewhere two other people could find it.

Casey: That's closer to the real distinction than big versus small. A small operation can be nimble and well-run. It can also be one retirement away from losing the only person who knew where the provenance file lives — and I promise you it's in a binder, and I promise you the binder is in the same building as the collection.

Riley: Next to the fire extinguisher it's meant to survive.

Casey: Exactly. Fireproof plan, flammable location.

Riley: And it's testable, which is the part I like. Does a small gallery or archive up here have a photographic inventory of its collection stored off-site, separate from the building? That's not a grant application question. It's a yes-or-no operational fact you could establish by Friday.

Casey: And if the answer's no, in most cases that isn't a funding gap. That's an afternoon with a decent camera and a hard drive that lives at somebody's house across town.

Riley: Which is what a Culture Days weekend is good for, past the studio tours — using the foot traffic to ask the people running the space whether that inventory exists, and if not, whether a volunteer with a phone could knock it out this month. Digital storytelling isn't only the polished VR reconstruction of a cave in Maharashtra. Sometimes it's four hundred flash photos of a collection, backed up twice.

Casey: The unglamorous version is usually the one that survives. And I'd take that question over another appeal for money, because the answer tells you something true about how the place is run rather than about its bank balance.

Riley: So the test for whether small is a virtue or an excuse up here comes down to whether the knowledge inside an organization is recorded anywhere besides one person's memory — and the good news is that's cheap, fast, and entirely within reach of a three-person board.
