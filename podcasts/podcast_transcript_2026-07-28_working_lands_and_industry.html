DEEP DIVE: CARIBOO CONNECTIONS - Working Lands & Industry

Riley: B.C. just opened a six-week engagement window on letting food processors build plants on Class 5 through 7 soil inside the Agricultural Land Reserve — land considered too poor for primary crop production — provided at least 5% of what gets processed there is grown in B.C. On the surface, that's using land nobody was farming to build capacity nobody's built. Forestry ran almost exactly this argument with beetle-killed salvage timber fifteen years ago: this land, this wood, isn't good for its original purpose anymore, so repurpose it. The question isn't whether the ALR proposal is good policy standing alone — it's what forestry's actual experience running that argument tells us about where it tends to go.

Casey: And the ending matters more than the opening pitch, so it's worth being precise about how it went. Mountain pine beetle salvage was framed as one-time, transitional cutting — dead wood nobody wanted, better to use it before it rotted in place. It kept several Cariboo mills running through the 2000s and into the early 2010s. But the annual allowable cut that got built around that salvage volume wasn't treated as temporary in practice. Tenure got reallocated on the strength of it, smaller operators got squeezed out as volume shrank, and now the allowable cut across the Interior is falling permanently, because there's no beetle wood left and the second growth isn't mature enough to harvest yet. Quesnel and Williams Lake both saw mill closures land on the back end of that curve.

Riley: The difference is that beetle salvage was a supply shock nobody actually chose — the wood was dying regardless of policy. The ALR proposal is a deliberate decision about which land to open, with a soil-class threshold and a made-in-B.C. sourcing requirement attached. Those are actual guardrails written into the proposal, not just an open-ended "it's marginal, take it."

Casey: Guardrails only hold if nobody downstream has an incentive to lean on them. BC Farms and Food has been warning for years that land can already be pulled out of the reserve entirely for industrial or commercial use through the existing exclusion process. Once "poor soil" justifies a processing plant, the pressure shifts to who gets to decide what counts as poor soil — that's the same mechanism that let forest licensees keep expanding harvest levels under salvage designations long after the original justification stopped applying to most of the volume being cut.

Riley: Except forest tenure and ALR exclusion run through completely different processes. Forest licences get allocated by the province directly to a small handful of major holders — Canfor, West Fraser, and Tolko control most of the Interior's harvesting rights, with essentially no public comment period attached. The ALR proposal at least has a defined six-week engagement window before anything's finalized, plus the sourcing requirement. That's not nothing.

Casey: A six-week comment period when the BC Chamber of Commerce has been actively lobbying for exactly this change since at least 2024. What does six weeks actually change if the industry association already got its ask written into the draft?

Riley: It changes who else gets heard before the regulation locks in — that matters even if the outcome still favours whoever asked first. Compare that to forest tenure, where there typically isn't any comment period at all when a major licence comes up for renewal. Quebec's new Filon fast-track for mining permits, and B.C.'s own appeals-process reforms, are both explicitly designed to remove deliberation steps, not add them.

Casey: So the ALR process at least keeps a deliberation step. Forest tenure doesn't have one to lose in the first place — and that's the bigger structural gap, because there's no press release to write about a process that quietly doesn't exist.

Riley: Which is exactly where Central Chilcotin Rehabilitation gets genuinely interesting, and not just as a feel-good name to drop next to a policy argument. They're not competing against the major licensees on a standard forest licence — the joint venture holds rehabilitation and biomass work tied directly into agreements with Tŝideldel, Tl'etinqox, and Yunesit'in. That's ownership sitting inside the tenure structure itself, not a contractor doing labour on someone else's cutblock and moving to the next job.

Casey: It's a genuinely different model, and it deserves to be taken seriously on its own terms — but it's small relative to the total allowable cut moving through the region every year. One Nation-led joint venture doing rehabilitation work doesn't offset a shrinking Interior cut being redistributed among three or four major licensees while mills keep closing on the margins.

Riley: Scale is the honest limitation, no argument. But the mechanism underneath it is replicable elsewhere — when expiring or returned harvesting volume comes up for reallocation anywhere in the Interior, the lever that matters is whether it defaults automatically back to the existing major licensees, or gets deliberately redirected toward community forest tenures and Nation-held agreements instead.
