Casey: If a government signed a treaty and then got sued over whether it kept the promise, can it rebuild trust through how it acts next? Or is it spending credit it inherited from the agreement? Start in the Yukon. The First Nation of Na-Cho Nyäk Dun filed a $150 million claim in Yukon Supreme Court on December 9th, 2025. The defendants are the Yukon government and the Government of Canada.

Riley: The claim alleges negligence in the 2024 heap leach failure at the Eagle Gold Mine. It also says the Yukon government authorized nearly every mining project proposed in its territory, and that this breached treaty rights. A final agreement is a legal contract with a court behind it. That's leverage a nation can actually use.

Casey: Leverage isn't trust. The Yukon and federal governments have asked the court to dismiss the case. The First Nation has also asked Canada's auditor general to investigate how the territory handled the incident. Both sides keep going to the same forums, and only one of them can ask a judge to end the case.

Riley: A dismissal motion tests whether the claim can proceed. It doesn't decide whether the claim is right.

Casey: Then I'd watch the mine sale more than the courtroom. CBC has reported that the mine's receiver has sued its contractor for negligence, and that a Singapore-based company has emerged as a suitor. The First Nation says it's neutral on the sale, but it has criticized the process. Who decides what happens to a mine after the operator fails? Control sits there.

Riley: With whoever holds the receivership file, and that's a court process, not a community one. The nation has standing in the lawsuit. It has a seat at the sale only if the process gives it one. Every filing is checkable, though. Trust in a fight like this gets built in the open, with dates attached.

Casey: Checkable isn't changed. An auditor general's report is public, and it doesn't come with a budget for follow-through. Who funds the second year of that review? Who reads the sale terms in year three, after the reporters have moved on?

Riley: The test is whether the record outlasts the news cycle. An audit request is a dated record of who was asked to act. The Yukon government can't later say it never heard it.

Casey: The record doesn't pay anyone. Operations stopped after a heap leach failure, and nothing I've seen says who pays for cleanup. Paper doesn't clean up a tailings pond.

Riley: True, but the receiver suing the contractor is a financial path toward cleanup money that doesn't depend on the Yukon's goodwill. Whether it's enough, nobody knows yet.

Casey: Nobody knows. I'd like that written down somewhere the next nation can find it.

Riley: Alberta shows the same problem on a smaller scale. Samson Cree Nation and Siksika ran short of ballots on Monday and had to request more. A rule for extended hours, written down before polling day. None of that needs a new law.
