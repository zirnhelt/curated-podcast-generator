Riley: Star Trek turned sixty years old this month. Fast Company ran a retrospective arguing the show basically invented half the technology we carry around now — communicators, tablets, universal translators, the whole list.

A company called Vocera built an actual hands-free voice badge for hospitals and emergency services, deployed it during COVID, made TIME's 100 Best Inventions list in 2020. Stryker bought Vocera in 2022 for just over three billion dollars.

Casey: So the badge is a real product with a real acquisition price. The Kirk-to-Cooper phone story is folklore.

Riley: Martin Cooper says decades later that Star Trek inspired the first handheld cellphone. There's no contemporaneous documentation tying it to the actual engineering at Motorola. Bell Labs and Motorola were already working the mobile phone problem in parallel with the show's rise. The story got attached afterward because it makes better copy than "R&D happens on its own timeline regardless of what's on TV."

Casey: Which is the more useful lesson. Engineers were building that phone whether or not Gene Roddenberry existed. What the show did was hand everyone a shape to imagine toward, and then absorb credit for an invention it didn't actually cause.

Riley: That's a version of who decides what's worth telling that's easy to miss entirely. It's not just who gets a platform. It's who gets credited for the idea after the fact. Vocera's engineers get a specific product, a specific acquisition, a specific dollar figure. Cooper gets a flattering myth that nobody bothers to check.

Casey: And nobody audits the myth, because checking it doesn't benefit anyone telling the story. The show looks prophetic. The inventor looks visionary. The retrospective article gets a better hook than "mobile phone R&D predates pop culture's spread by a decade."
