DEEP DIVE: CARIBOO CONNECTIONS - Arts, Culture & Digital Storytelling

Riley: If a band plays a full set in a hall in Wells, or Horsefly, and nobody records a single second of it, did that show still happen as far as anyone outside the room is concerned?

Casey: It happened for the eighty people who were there. Whether it happened for anyone else is a separate question, and I don't think the answer's obvious.

Riley: The field-recording piece in The Verge gets at this from a strange angle. There's a platform called Earth Garden built around ambient audio — brooks, forests, city streets — recorded all over the world and served up as background sound for sleep or focus work. Anyone can browse recordings by region.

Casey: So a field recording made at a creek outside Horsefly could end up as white noise for a stranger in Berlin who has no idea what they're listening to, or where it came from.

Riley: A thirty-second clip can travel further than most of the people who made it ever will.

Casey: Reach isn't the same as being known, though. Earth Garden strips a place down to pure texture — water sound, no name attached, no context, no credit line back to whoever stood there with a microphone for an afternoon. The platform extracts value from the recording. The place gets nothing measurable back.

Riley: That's true of most recordings, though. A photograph of the Grand Canyon doesn't credit the specific ledge somebody was standing on when they took it.

Casey: The Grand Canyon has ten thousand other ways of being known to the world. A creek outside Horsefly mostly doesn't. When the only version of a place that exists anywhere online is an anonymous ambient loop, that recording ends up doing all the representing for it, and it was never built to represent anything. It was built to help somebody sleep.

Riley: Fair distinction. The recording exists somewhere in the world. The place still isn't findable through it.

Casey: Right. Existing and being legible aren't the same thing.

Riley: Where this gets more interesting is when a community controls the recording tool instead of just supplying raw material for somebody else's app. Adafruit wrote up a project last week where a hobbyist built a full 512-LED stage lighting matrix driven by a modded Nintendo Switch — pixel art streamed live over a USB connection to an ESP32 controller running the light show in real time. Total build cost is a fraction of renting a commercial stage lighting rig for a night.

Casey: Who fixes it when a firmware update from Nintendo breaks the homebrew runtime the whole thing depends on?

Riley: Somebody with a soldering iron and some patience, same as anyone maintaining analog gear. But look at what the barrier used to be — a small venue wanting real stage production had to fly in equipment and a technician from Kamloops or Vancouver for a single show. Now it's a used console, an ESP32 board that costs a few dollars, and a weekend of setup.

Casey: A weekend, and then a maintainer for as long as that rig keeps getting used. A couple months back we talked about a motion-capture tool with the same problem — cheap access doesn't automatically mean anyone sticks around to run the thing after opening night.

Riley: It doesn't have to be the same person forever. It needs a venue that treats the gear as standing infrastructure, not a one-off novelty somebody got excited about for a single gig.

Casey: And that's the gap none of the arts organizations around here have closed. Somebody gets excited about a lighting rig, it works great for one show, and then it sits in a closet because nobody was ever assigned to own it.

Riley: The digital ID story is the flip side of that — a case where the technology gets imposed on a venue from outside instead of something the venue chose and can maintain itself. TechRadar reported that pubs and other venues in the UK can now accept digital ID apps for age verification at the door. The writer flagged a specific problem: the scanning step is making lines longer, not shorter, because the app adds friction that flashing a physical card never had.

Casey: Picture that at a hundred-person show in a Quesnel legion hall on a Friday night in February. One phone, one person running the scanner app, and a line stretching out into minus fifteen weather.

Riley: It's not mandatory here yet. But it's the pattern worth watching — one compliance problem gets "solved" with an app, and the app becomes a new bottleneck nobody budgeted staff time to handle.

Casey: That's a recurring failure with a lot of rural tech adoption generally. The tool gets designed assuming a venue with a dedicated door staff of six people. A volunteer-run hall has one person doing tickets, running the bar, and now also supposed to be running ID verification.

Riley: Somebody ought to just staple a laminated sign to the door that says "phone dead, use a card," and call the whole debate solved.

Casey: That would actually work better than half the pilot programs I've read about.

Riley: So the honest read is that better documentation tools and worse gatekeeping tools are showing up side by side, and a small venue has to sort out which is which with no IT department to consult and no budget for a trial period.

Casey: And no guarantee that sorting happens at all before somebody just adopts whatever a sales rep happens to be pitching that month.

Riley: Which loops back to the recording question. A guitar's a useful comparison here — a while back we covered the story of a BC couple who tracked down and reunited a missing guitar with musician Randy Bachman decades after it disappeared. No recording did that. No app did that. It was one physical object with a documented history, and people who cared enough to chase it down.

Casey: A physical instrument carries its own record built in — scratches, repairs, who owned it and when. A digital file doesn't carry any of that unless somebody deliberately attaches the context and keeps attaching it every time the file moves.

Riley: So maybe the answer to whether a show existed isn't really about capture at all. It's about whether anything survives afterward with the story still attached to it — a recording with a name on it, an instrument with a paper trail, even just a printed program from that night.

Casey: The printed program is underrated. The Museum of the Cariboo Chilcotin has rodeo records and ranching artifacts going back generations because somebody kept paper, not because anyone was running a microphone.

Riley: So here's a concrete version of tonight's argument. A hall like the Arts Station in Wells could own one decent field recorder and treat "record the show" as a standing job description, the same way someone's assigned to unlock the doors or run the bar. Not a grant application. A line in a job description.

Casey: That's a smaller ask than most solutions get pitched at, and it survives past year one, because it doesn't depend on a funding cycle. It depends on whoever runs the venue deciding, once, that it matters.

Riley: It also partly answers the Earth Garden problem. If the venue holds its own recording, the name and the date and the place stay attached to it. Nobody's stripping it down into anonymous ambient wallpaper for someone else's sleep app three years later.

Casey: It still doesn't answer who decides what gets kept and what gets deleted five years from now when the hard drive fills up and somebody has to choose. That's a governance question, not a hardware problem, and it doesn't get solved by buying a better recorder.

Riley: True. But an unmaintained hard drive with the wrong two hundred hours on it is still a better problem to have than a show that was never captured at all.
